# Performance Research — Adversarial Appraisal

**Measurement bar** enumerates mechanisms that force a performance claim to be grounded; **Folklore** lists unmeasured performance advice LLMs reproduce reflexively, which the agent is explicitly forbidden to emit; **Rules that stick** covers evidence on what makes an agent-facing rule actually get followed.

## Verdicts

- **Treating repo-level optimization benchmark results (GSO <5% opt@1; SWE-Perf 1.28–2.26% vs expert 10.85%; SWE-fficiency 0.225x mean Speedup Ratio) as the design constraint for a performance agent**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Three independent teams (GSO/NeurIPS 2025 D&B, SWE-Perf/TikTok, SWE-fficiency) using different repos, different metrics, and different harnesses converge on the same conclusion: frontier models extract roughly one fifth to one twentieth of expert speedup on real codebases. GSO's opt@0 vs opt@0.95 split (45–70% pass tests, <5% reach 95% of expert speedup) and SWE-fficiency's outcome breakdown (best model: 47% of patches SLOWER than pre-edit) are the two most load-bearing numbers in the entire corpus. This is not a footnote to soften — it means the null hypothesis for any unmeasured optimization suggestion is that it is worthless or harmful.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **PIE-style competitive-programming optimization results (6.86x mean speedup, beating the 3.66x human mean) as evidence that LLMs can optimize code**
  - *Verdict:* contradicted-by-evidence
  - *Reasoning:* PIE is single-file C++ with dense hot loops, deterministic gem5 measurement, and no localization problem — it removes precisely the three things that make repo work hard. The same capability class scores 2.26% end-to-end gain on SWE-Perf and 0.225x expert SR on SWE-fficiency. The gap is roughly two orders of magnitude. PIE is a valid result about its own setting and an invalid prior for a repository agent; importing it sets confidence ~100x too high. Both research bundles flagged this independently and both are right.
  - *Adopt:* reject
- **Static/grep-based bottleneck localization — the agent reads a diff or greps for suspicious patterns and asserts which code is hot**
  - *Verdict:* contradicted-by-evidence
  - *Reasoning:* This is the specific step models are measurably worst at, and it is the step a diff-reading reviewer implicitly claims to perform. SWE-fficiency: 68% of expert gains live in functions the LM never edits, despite hitting the right file 55% of the time — file-level localization is not function-level localization. GSO names bottleneck localization as a top-three failure mode even when handed the performance test as a precise spec. JETO-Bench: 22.0% of patches at an outright wrong location plus 27.5% right location wrong semantics. Gregg's 'streetlight' anti-method names the mechanism: grep is the streetlight, and off-CPU blocking, lock contention, and GC pauses are structurally invisible to it.
  - *Adopt:* reject
- **Putting 'profile before optimizing' / 'measure first, never guess' in the system prompt and treating the problem as solved**
  - *Verdict:* contradicted-by-evidence
  - *Reasoning:* Directly ablated: PerfAgent's prompt-only profiler variant scored 17.6% opt@1 on GSO versus 44.1% when the harness actually runs the profiler and injects condensed hotspot output — 'agents cannot reliably self-direct profiler usage without system orchestration.' Caveat I want on the record: this is ONE unreplicated July-2026 preprint carrying the field's largest claimed effect, so the specific numbers are directional. But the direction is corroborated structurally by every artifact that works (Scalene, Optimo, MOA all have the profiler select the target) and by every artifact that doesn't (wshobson's 'Establish performance baseline with comprehensive measurement and profiling' with no tool check anywhere in the file). The soft version of this instruction is satisfied by the model merely asserting it considered performance.
  - *Adopt:* reject
- **Hard tool-availability gate at the top of the workflow with a defined STOP action (Anthropic web-perf skill)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* I verified this file verbatim at /Users/kyeshmz/.claude/skills/web-perf/SKILL.md line 20. No published outcome data exists for it. But it is the only artifact I examined that structurally cannot emit an unmeasured finding, because every finding shape is a field of a trace result. The mechanism is a control-flow branch rather than an exhortation, which is the whole difference between it and the popular agent files. Adopt on cost-asymmetry grounds: the failure mode it prevents (confident static perf narration) is exactly the one the benchmarks document, and the cost of the gate is one tool call.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Profiler-in-the-loop with measured feedback, objective-driven controller, and best-correct-patch selection (PerfAgent architecture)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* The single most consequential design claim in the corpus (GSO 19.6%→39.2%, SWE-fficiency-Lite 26%→74%, with clean ablations: loop-only 29.4%, +profiler 34.4%, full 39.2%) rests entirely on one self-reported, unreplicated preprint from three weeks ago. Treat every number as directional. What survives independent of PerfAgent: GSO separately found inference-time sampling plateaus at opt@10 ≈12–15%; Scalene, Optimo, and MOA independently converge on profiler-selects-target/LLM-proposes-edit; and ECCO independently found execution feedback preserves correctness. Adopt the architecture, cite the numbers as unreplicated.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Scaling by sampling k candidate patches and picking the best (best@k with oracle selection)**
  - *Verdict:* contradicted-by-evidence
  - *Reasoning:* Weaker and 3–4x more expensive than measured iteration. OpenHands best@5 with oracle selection: 26.5% opt@1 at $11.01/task; PerfAgent's 5-turn measured loop: 44.1% at $2.88. Independently, GSO found opt@10 plateaus at 12–15% with diminishing returns past 8 samples, and that 8 rollouts x 50 steps beats 1 rollout x 400 steps — so parallelism helps only up to a low ceiling. Sampling buys diversity of guesses, not knowledge of where the time goes. The one caveat: the cost comparison is again single-source (PerfAgent), while the plateau is peer-reviewed (GSO).
  - *Adopt:* reject
- **MOA-style narrow codified anti-patterns with generated checkers (13 named memory anti-patterns, 769 patches, 92.5% expert acceptance on 100M+ LOC)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* This is the strongest positive result in the corpus and the main empirical case for the rules-and-skills mandate — but the headline framing (92.5% vs open-ended review's 36.4%) compares two unrelated studies with different codebases, different reviewers, different languages, and different definitions of acceptance. That is not a controlled comparison, and it is being used to support a ~2.5x claim. What is solid: one industrial deployment showed that constraining the solution space to named patterns with mechanical detectors produced acceptance rates a general reviewer does not approach, on a single C/C++ codebase, for a single problem class (memory bloat/churn). Adopt the FORMAT (name + detector + canonical fix) as the target shape for every durable rule; do not cite the 92.5%-vs-36.4% delta as a measured effect of narrowing.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Two-stage generate-then-validate review with a precision-over-recall gate (PostHog ReviewHog)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* Verified both halves verbatim on disk. No published outcome data. Structurally it is the right shape when the generator is a confident bullshitter: decouple cheap noisy recall from one expensive precision gate. Two things worth naming adversarially. First, the perf perspective on its own is a false-positive factory — its N+1 'detection' is `rg "for.*in|while" -A 10 | rg "query|select|fetch"`, which finds syntactic co-occurrence, and it explicitly instructs 'report every performance or reliability issue you find.' Lifting that half without the gate gives you the noise with no filter. Second, and more important for calibration: the gate's bar is 'name the concrete trigger AND the concrete consequence' — an argument-quality bar, not a measurement bar. Even the best shipped practitioner artifact does not require measurement.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **SWE-Perf's three-tier apply→correctness→performance gate with a statistical protocol (20 reps + warm-up, IQR k=1, Mann-Whitney U p<0.1)**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Peer-reviewed (ICML 2026), affordable in CI, and it is the operational definition of 'measured' that makes the field's other results comparable. The structural insight is the ordering: performance is only evaluated on patches that already passed correctness, which forces the joint claim. Its own results justify the gate — correctness after model optimization patches ran 42–84%, meaning a sixth to over half of 'optimizations' broke behavior depending on model.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Rigorous repetition + significance + minimum-effect-size protocol (JETO-Bench: 30 runs, warm-up, p≤0.10/≥5% default and p≤0.05/≥10% conservative)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* The paper itself is an unverified 2026 preprint, but the protocol it embodies is convergent across four independent sources (SWE-Perf's 20+IQR+Mann-Whitney, Criterion's bootstrapped t-test plus noise floor, hyperfine's ≥10 runs/≥3s, Optimo's ≥10% opt% threshold). Its most useful contribution is the humbling number: of 102 executable REAL HUMAN performance-improving patches, only 19 showed statistically significant improvement under the loose regime and 8 under the conservative one — with the authors warning that many apparent wins were 'noise in time collection rather than actual performance testing.' If human perf patches mostly fail a proper significance test, an agent's single-run claim is not evidence of anything.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Abandoning wall-clock for deterministic work counting (COFFE's CPU instruction counts; PIE's gem5; CodSpeed-style instruction counting in CI)**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Four independent sources converge from different directions: COFFE (FSE 2025, peer-reviewed) abandoned wall clock as 'not stable and comprehensive, threatening the validity of the time efficiency evaluation'; PIE ran everything in gem5 specifically to eliminate hardware variance; Bencher measured >30% run-to-run variance on GitHub Action Runners vs <2% bare metal; Criterion ships a configurable noise floor because sub-threshold deltas are meaningless. The practical rule — count work (instructions, allocations, syscalls, queries) for small units, reserve wall clock for workloads long enough to exceed noise — is as well supported as anything here.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Setting a wall-clock CI performance budget as a fixed percentage (5–10%) on shared runners**
  - *Verdict:* contradicted-by-evidence
  - *Reasoning:* Below the noise floor by a wide margin. GitHub Action Runners show >30% run-to-run variance; the median real optimization an agent produces is 1–2%. The gate fires constantly, gets muted or made non-blocking within weeks, and afterward the repo has something that looks like coverage and provides none. Note github-action-benchmark's default alert-threshold is 200% — deliberately loose, because the maintainers know this. The Bencher variance figures are vendor-sourced (one caveat), but the direction is corroborated by Criterion's and JMH's existence.
  - *Adopt:* reject
- **Amdahl payoff arithmetic before any suggestion — payoff = (measured fraction of end-to-end time in region) x (plausible regional speedup), with a hard floor (Gregg's Method R)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* No controlled study measures this discipline in an agent. It is the single cheapest structural filter available, because computing the denominator forces the agent to discover that most of its true-but-irrelevant findings are worth 0.3% before it emits them. The shape of a finding that clears the bar is well documented (Node's official walkthrough: 51.8% of CPU in one PBKDF2 frame → 5.33→19.46 req/s), and the local web-perf skill already ships the degenerate case ('Don't prioritize changes with 0ms impact'). Adopt because it costs one line and converts 'optimize hot paths' from slogan to gate.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Cold-path filter — no perf finding permitted on code that runs once (startup, migrations, CLI parsing, fixtures, error branches)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* Cheapest suggestion-spam filter available and it directly encodes the actual (untruncated) Knuth position. One adversarial correction: the research bundle claims this is 'mechanically decidable from the call graph without any measurement,' which is false in general — static reachability and call-frequency are undecidable in dynamic languages with reflection, DI, dynamic dispatch, and metaprogramming. Adopt it as a required declaration ('state the execution frequency and how you determined it') rather than as a decision procedure, so an unverifiable frequency claim lowers confidence instead of silently passing.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Query-count assertions and runnable N+1 detectors (PlanetScale's performance_schema digest query; Django's profile-then-select_related/prefetch_related ordering)**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* The rare category where the win is arithmetically predictable before measurement (N round trips become 1–2, and round-trip cost dominates at any realistic N), the detection is a runnable command rather than an inference, and the fix is checkable by an assertion that prevents regression. Multiple independent practitioners converge: PlanetScale ships a literal detection SQL query, Django's official docs order the remedies, ReviewHog names N+1 as its canonical keep-example. Contrast sharply with grep-based 'loop near the word query' inference, which is co-occurrence, not evidence.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Composite index leading-column rule with mandatory query citation, selectivity, and write-path cost**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Deterministic, checkable without running a database, and convergent across a database vendor's own skill (PlanetScale: 'equality first, then range/sort'), a canonical practitioner reference (use-the-index-luke's phone-book argument), and Django's docs. The write-cost half is what stops index advice from being treated as free — 'the fewer indexes a table has, the better the insert, delete and update performance.' An index recommendation without a named query and a leading-column justification is the most common form of confident, wrong perf review.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Efficiency-gap benchmarks establishing that LLM-generated code is slow by default and that efficiency does not ride the correctness scaling curve (EffiBench, Mercury, EvalPerf, ECCO, COFFE)**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Five peer-reviewed benchmarks, independently constructed, converging: GPT-4 code averages 3.12x canonical human runtime (worst case 13.89x time / 43.92x memory); Mercury shows Pass 65% vs Beyond <50%; EvalPerf shows model scaling improves correctness but not efficiency; ECCO shows 'most methods degrade functional correctness and moderately increase program efficiency.' Two consequences. First, construction-time rules have unusually high leverage on an AI-written codebase specifically, because there is more recoverable slack (Optimo recovers up to 13.51x on LLM-generated code vs 3.97x on human code). Second, a model upgrade is not a substitute for a measurement harness — scaffold deltas in this corpus dwarf model deltas.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Benchmark-gaming / reward-hacking mitigation (time the first run only; flag caches keyed on benchmark fixtures; forbid harness edits)**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Independently confirmed by two sources approaching from opposite directions: GSO had to engineer a dedicated Hack Detector penalizing 'memoization, harness hijacking' — you do not build anti-cheat for a rare problem — and PerfAgent's ablation flagged 18 high-performing patches of which 14 were result caching exploiting repeated timing loops, reduced to 3 by timing the first run only. The generalization from the local `improve` skill is exact: 'Executors game criteria — a test that asserts nothing meaningful passes and proves nothing.' Any measurement gate creates an incentive to game it, and caching adjacent to the timing loop is the dominant strategy. The check must be structural (did the diff touch the harness; does the cache key derive from the fixture), not numeric, because the benchmark genuinely got faster.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Forbidding self-generated benchmark workloads; requiring workload provenance from production data, an existing benchmark, or a human**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* Single-source but directly measured and mechanistically clear: SWE-fficiency found human-annotated workloads beat LM-generated workloads 76% of the time, and 47% of LM-generated workloads showed no significant speedup for ANY edit — meaning the agent optimizes against a workload incapable of demonstrating improvement, then reports success. Corroborated in spirit by EvalPerf, whose entire contribution is generating computationally expensive inputs because 'simplistic test inputs' cannot reveal efficiency, and by GSO supplying the performance test as a precise spec rather than asking the agent to invent one. The subtle corollary matters as much: a flat result is not proof of no improvement — 209 of 308 non-significant tasks in the Rethinking study had real improvements masked by inadequate inputs.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Agentic code review acceptance in the wild as the baseline the consultant must beat (CodeRabbit: 36.4% accepted / 56.3% rejected across 31,073 comments, 239 repos; Martian: ~52% precision for a top-3 tool)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* The CodeRabbit study is a single July-2026 preprint but with unusually large n and a hard acceptance signal; the Martian number is vendor-published and self-favorable, so treat it as directional only. Together they are the ONLY direct measurement of review-comment quality in the entire corpus — everything else measures patch-writing agents. That matters enormously and neither research bundle flagged it clearly enough: transferring 'SWE-fficiency's best model produced slower patches 47% of the time' into 'roughly half of review findings are wrong' is an inference across a task boundary, not a measurement. It cuts both ways — a reviewer might do better (it never has to implement) or worse (it usually cannot measure at all). Adopt 36.4% acceptance as the explicit target metric in place of finding count; do not present the transfer as measured.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Confidence-tiered findings where LOW confidence produces an 'investigate' plan rather than a 'fix' plan (local `improve` skill)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* Verified verbatim on disk (audit-playbook line 119). No outcome data. It solves a real problem that a pure drop rule creates: the reviewer often notices the right thing without being able to prove it, and a hard precision gate discards that signal entirely. Downgrading routes the suspicion into the one activity that can resolve it — measurement — and structurally prevents an unmeasured suspicion from becoming an unmeasured code change. Pairs naturally with the localization evidence: since the agent is measurably bad at asserting where time goes, most of its findings SHOULD land in the investigate tier, and a prompt that makes that the honest default is more useful than one that forces binary keep/drop.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Language/abstraction-level scoping with escalation instead of guessing on native and GPU code (PerfCodeBench: Python 85.53% CRR, C 45.16%, CUDA 10.53%; GPT-5.4 68.67% CPU CGRE vs 0.00% GPU)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* Unverified 2026 preprint, but corroborated by GSO's independent qualitative findings (o4-mini omitted required C changes 40% of the time; Claude-3.5-v2 made unnecessary low-level changes in Python-only tasks causing segfaults). The escalation form matters more than the thresholds: with profiler support PerfAgent crossed into C/C++/Cython/Rust on 48% of GSO instances vs 31% baseline and lifted non-Python success 11.7%→31.7% — so the correct rule is 'report the localization and request profiling,' not 'stay silent below the managed-language boundary.' Unprofiled native suggestions are how a performance review becomes a crash.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Tail-aware metric naming — requiring each finding to name whether it improves throughput, p50, p99/p99.9, memory ceiling, or cost (Dean & Barroso)**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Peer-reviewed with measured deployment results at Google (tied requests: 16% median and 40% p99.9 latency reduction; micro-partitioning at ~20 partitions/machine shedding load in 5% increments). The motivating arithmetic is the best single teaching number available: a component at 10ms typical / 1s p99 fanned out to 100 servers puts 63% of user requests over one second. Independently reinforced by Core Web Vitals being assessed at p75 rather than the mean. This closes the most common category error in perf review — optimizing the average in a system whose SLO is the tail, or adding a cache that improves p50 and worsens p99 on the miss path.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Profile-guided optimization and 'check whether the compiler/runtime/framework already does it, and name the version you checked' (Go PGO 2–7%; React Compiler auto-memoization)**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Go's PGO is officially documented with measured results (2–7% CPU typical; worked example -3.83%, p=0.000, n=40) and is a better first recommendation than any hand optimization of comparable size. React Compiler explicitly eliminates 'the need for manual useMemo, useCallback, and React.memo.' A large fraction of LLM perf folklore is advice that was true for a runtime three to eight years ago, and a version check converts a stale rule into either a live finding or a deletion. Keep Skarupke's counter-evidence honest though: modern compilers still miss obvious transformations, so 'the compiler handles it' must be verified per case, not assumed.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **ETH Zurich finding: context files do not generally improve task success while raising inference cost >20%; instructions are followed, repository overviews are not**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* The most rigorous study in the entire corpus about the rules half of the mandate, and the one that most directly contradicts what agent vendors recommend. Controlled across multiple LLMs, multiple agents, and BOTH LLM-generated and developer-committed files — so 'a human wrote it' is not the fix. Read precisely: this is not 'rules don't work.' It is 'descriptive context is net-negative and the typical context file nets to zero at a >20% cost premium, while imperative non-obvious instructions are followed well.' Independently operationalized by Claude Code's own /doctor, which trims content derivable from the codebase and keeps pitfalls, rationale, and conventions that differ from tool defaults. The design consequence is severe: default answer to 'should I write a rule?' is no.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **ACE-style delta-updated playbook — append/edit/prune individual bullets, never regenerate the rules file wholesale**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* Measured (+10.6% on agent benchmarks, +8.6% domain, 86.9% lower adaptation latency) but on agent/reasoning benchmarks, not on code performance work — transfer is an assumption. The actionable part is the negative control, which is the sharpest single number in the whole research set: accumulated context of 18,282 tokens at 66.7% accuracy, collapsed by ONE monolithic LLM rewrite to 122 tokens at 57.1% — below the 63.7% no-context baseline. Context collapse is a cliff, not a slope, and 'ask the model to clean up and rewrite the rules file' is the exact trigger. Adopt the append-and-prune discipline; the measured failure is what justifies it.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Escalating durable knowledge from prose rule to deterministic enforcement (Semgrep rule with ruleid/ok test pair → CI benchmark threshold → PreToolUse/PostToolUse hook)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* No study measures speedups from agent-authored lint rules; the argument is structural, and honestly so. But the structural argument is strong and vendor-confirmed: Anthropic's own docs state 'Settings rules are enforced by the client regardless of what Claude decides to do. CLAUDE.md instructions shape Claude's behavior but are not a hard enforcement layer,' plus 'if the instruction is something that must run at a specific point... write it as a hook instead.' Docs also confirm subagents don't inherit auto memory and nested CLAUDE.md isn't re-injected after compaction — which is exactly the regime a long optimization session runs in. The load-bearing detail is the NEGATIVE test (`# ok:`): a rule shipped with only a positive test is over-broad, fires on correct code, and gets disabled within weeks, losing the codified knowledge entirely.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Glob-scoped rule files (Claude Code `paths:`, Cursor `globs:`, Copilot `applyTo:`, Windsurf glob mode, Cline `paths:`) instead of always-on instruction files**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* No head-to-head measurement of scoped vs unscoped rules exists. The motivation is entirely from the measured cost side (ETH's >20% premium applies on every task including irrelevant ones) plus a mechanism disclosed by the harness itself: content is injected with 'this context may or may not be relevant to your tasks. You should not respond to this context unless it is highly relevant' — so a non-universal rule in the always-on file is actively invited to be dropped. One concrete trap worth encoding: a plain .md file in .cursor/rules is silently ignored because it lacks frontmatter, so emitting one format and assuming portability produces dead files.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **The '150–200 instructions before compliance degrades, ~50 spent by the harness' budget**
  - *Verdict:* popular-but-unsupported
  - *Reasoning:* Asserted in a blog post without cited methodology — folklore with a number attached, which is the most dangerous kind because it launders a guess as a measurement. The adjacent figures are better grounded and should be used instead: an analysis of 2,500+ AGENTS.md repos found median well-performing files at ~300–350 words with diminishing returns past 500 and NEGATIVE correlation past 1,000; Anthropic states 'target under 200 lines per CLAUDE.md. Longer files consume more context and reduce adherence'; Cursor says under 500 lines; Copilot says 'no longer than 2 pages'; Windsurf enforces 12,000 chars per workspace rule and 6,000 global. Keep the hard-cap DISCIPLINE (a numeric cap forces ranking, which is the actual scarce skill); drop the specific 150–200 figure.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Capability-taxonomy and noun-phrase-checklist performance agents (VoltAgent, wshobson performance-engineer and database-optimizer)**
  - *Verdict:* popular-but-unsupported
  - *Reasoning:* Reject outright, and one of these is actively harmful rather than merely useless. These files are vocabulary lists — 150 of VoltAgent's 287 lines are verbless noun phrases ('CPU profiling / Memory analysis / I/O investigation'), and wshobson's is a taxonomy of tool brand names. Nothing in them can be complied with or violated, so nothing constrains anything; the model treats bullets as headings to fill with plausible prose, which maximizes suggestion count at zero measurement. The genuinely dangerous part is VoltAgent's fabricated results template ('Improved response time by 68% (2.1s to 0.67s)... increased throughput by 245%') and pre-filled progress JSON: it demonstrates to the model that emitting specific-looking percentages IS the deliverable, with no step anywhere in the file that produces a number. Never ship an example output containing a measurement whose provenance isn't also shown.
  - *Adopt:* reject
- **Deliverable-is-a-document orchestration (wshobson's 13-step performance-optimization command)**
  - *Verdict:* popular-but-unsupported
  - *Reasoning:* Split the verdict. The process engineering is genuinely good and worth stealing: state.json checkpointing, 'Read from prior step files — do NOT rely on context window memory,' mandatory human checkpoints, halt-on-failure. What it wraps is worthless: every one of the 13 steps ends with 'Write your complete X report as a single markdown document,' and step 1 says 'Generate flame graphs for CPU usage, heap dumps for memory analysis' with no command, no tool, and no availability check. The result is 13 markdown files describing an investigation that never happened, where each step reads the previous step's PROSE as data, so a hallucinated bottleneck in step 1 gains confidence as it propagates. Its success criteria ('Cost Efficiency: Performance per dollar improved by minimum 30%') are constants the agent cannot evaluate. Steal the scaffolding; replace the payload with tool invocations.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Generic constant SLO thresholds copy-pasted into the prompt ('P50 < 200ms, P95 < 1s, P99 < 2s', 'Query P95 < 100ms')**
  - *Verdict:* popular-but-unsupported
  - *Reasoning:* Numbers from nowhere read as rigor. An agent handed these flags a batch job's 400ms endpoint as a violation and passes a checkout path at 190ms. Thresholds are only meaningful when they are standardized for the metric (Core Web Vitals is the legitimate case — web-perf's table is fine because web.dev defines it at p75) or read from the repo's own budget file. The correct instruction is to fetch or derive the threshold and cite its source, which web-perf already models: 'Prefer retrieval over pre-training when citing specific numbers.'
  - *Adopt:* reject
- **Five-principle structural grading of emitted rules (Success Definition / Assessment Rubric / Scope Boundary / Data Classification / Quality Gate)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* Weak evidence, useful artifact. n=34, LLM-as-judge, no inter-rater kappa, and — the paper says so itself — it measures structural PRESENCE of prompt features, not downstream agent performance; a structurally complete prompt can still contain wrong content. What makes it worth adopting anyway is Appendix A's literal exemplar text at each score level, which is directly reusable as a self-check. The Data Classification principle in particular (weakest in the corpus at mean 0.34 — almost nobody writes it) is the closest thing in the literature to a drop-in fix for the central problem: 'Mark inferences explicitly as Inferred from [source]. Do not assert facts you cannot trace to a specific file and line.'
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Having the consultant (or a same-family model) validate its own performance findings without an external artifact**
  - *Verdict:* contradicted-by-evidence
  - *Reasoning:* Correlated errors in homogeneous LLM pipelines echo rather than cancel — when the generating and reviewing agents reason from the same artifact and share a training distribution, review confirms rather than catches. Caveat: the paper making this argument explicitly says its experiments use 'a planted bug corpus rather than a natural defect sample; they are directional evidence, not a controlled demonstration.' But the effect is independently visible in the perf benchmarks: models satisfice at the first measurable win (SWE-fficiency median trajectory under 50 of 100 allowed actions) and GSO catalogues 'lazy optimization' in ~30% of o4-mini trajectories — self-assessment terminates early because the model is checking against its own standard. Require an external artifact (profile, semgrep --test pass, benchmark run) as the acceptance gate for anything promoted to a durable rule.
  - *Adopt:* reject
- **Framing the deliverable as a code-reviewing agent that reads diffs and emits performance findings**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* The uncomfortable meta-verdict neither research bundle states plainly. Every architecture with measured success (PerfAgent, Scalene, Optimo, MOA) has the same shape: a profiler or detector selects the target, and the LLM proposes an edit for an already-indicted location. A pure reviewer inverts this — it asks the model to perform localization, the step benchmarks show it is worst at (68% of expert gains in functions LMs never touch), using grep, the tool Gregg names as the streetlight. Meanwhile the only direct measurements of review-comment quality (36.4% acceptance, ~52% precision) are unflattering. This does not mean don't build it; it means the honest default output of a diff-reading perf consultant is investigation tasks and codified detectors, not fixes — and any finding it cannot ground in tool output should be labeled a hypothesis requiring measurement rather than a finding.
  - *Adopt:* adapt

## Convergent wisdom

- **Passing correctness tests is nearly uncorrelated with being faster — the correctness funnel and the performance funnel come apart almost completely, so 'tests are green' is not evidence about performance.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - GSO (NeurIPS 2025 D&B): opt@0 45–70% pass functional tests vs <5% reaching 95% of expert speedup
    - SWE-Perf (ICML 2026): Gemini-2.5-Pro applies 95.00% of patches with 83.57% correctness and delivers 1.48% gain against expert 10.85%
    - SWE-fficiency: best model has only 9% test failures yet 47% of its patches are slower than pre-edit
    - PerfCodeBench: correctness and efficiency shown to be decoupled axes (GPT-5 lower CRR than GPT-5.4 but higher CGRE, 73.99% vs 66.27%)
- **Frontier models extract roughly one fifth or less of expert speedup on real repository optimization tasks, and a large share of their patches are regressions.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - SWE-Perf: best repo-level method 2.26% vs expert 10.85%
    - SWE-fficiency: best model mean Speedup Ratio 0.225x; 47% of patches slower than pre-edit
    - GSO: <5% opt@1 against a 95%-of-expert threshold, ~12–15% plateau at opt@10
    - JETO-Bench (Java): OpenHands 14.3% correct fixes, 36.3% no patch generated
- **Bottleneck localization is the hard part and the step models are worst at — file-level localization is not function-level localization.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - SWE-fficiency: 68% of expert gains in functions the LM never edits, despite hitting the right file 55% of the time
    - GSO: 'challenges in accurately localizing bottlenecks' named as a primary failure mode; >60% of failed trajectories made ≤15% of the human commit's edits
    - JETO-Bench: 22.0% wrong location outright, plus 27.5% right location with wrong semantics
    - Brendan Gregg: the 'streetlight' anti-method describes exactly the grep-driven failure
- **Wall-clock timing is unreliable at the granularity agents work at; deterministic work counting or a proper statistical protocol is required before any speedup claim.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - COFFE (FSE 2025): abandons wall clock for CPU instruction counts as 'not stable and comprehensive'
    - PIE: runs everything in the gem5 simulator specifically to eliminate hardware variance
    - Bencher: >30% run-to-run variance on GitHub Action Runners vs <2% bare metal
    - JETO-Bench: even with 30 runs + warm-up, only 8 of 102 real human perf patches survived p≤0.05/≥10%
    - hyperfine: ships outlier detection because 'interference from other programs and caching effects' routinely swamp deltas
    - Criterion.rs: configurable noise threshold below which changes are classified as noise and ignored
- **Models reach for a stereotyped shortcut menu — caching/memoization, identity checks, input-specific fast paths, compiler flags, import and environment tweaks — precisely where experts make structural and semantic changes.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - SWE-fficiency: 'shortcut bias — models prefer identity checks, fast-paths, and memoization over structural improvements experts employ'
    - GSO: 'lazy optimization strategies' instantiated as -O3 on already-optimized builds and input-specific fast paths that fail generalization (~30% of o4-mini trajectories)
    - SWE-Perf: 'LLMs focus more on low-level code structures (e.g., imports, environment setup), while experts target high-level semantic abstractions'
- **Adding a measurement gate creates a predictable exploit, and the dominant strategy is caching across repeated timing iterations — so anti-gaming checks must be structural, not numeric.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - GSO: built a dedicated Hack Detector penalizing 'deceptive optimizations (e.g., memoization, harness hijacking)'
    - PerfAgent: 18 flagged high-performing patches, 14 involving result caching exploiting repeated timing loops; timing-first-run-only cut hacks 18→3
    - Local `improve` skill: 'Executors game criteria — a test that asserts nothing meaningful passes and proves nothing'
- **Models satisfice — they stop at the first measurable win with most of their budget unspent, forfeiting the majority of achievable gain.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - SWE-fficiency: median trajectory under 50 actions against a 100-action budget, explicitly attributed to submitting on first measurable speedup
    - GSO: 'lazy optimization strategies' named in ~30% of o4-mini trajectories
    - PerfAgent: per-turn harmonic-mean speedup on GSO climbing 2.5x→4.0x→5.2x→6.0x→6.4x across five turns
- **LLM-generated code is substantially slower than human canonical code by default, and efficiency does not improve with model scale the way correctness does.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - EffiBench: GPT-4 code averages 3.12x canonical execution time; worst case 13.89x time and 43.92x memory across 42 models
    - Mercury: leading code LLMs at Pass 65% but Beyond <50%
    - EvalPerf: model scaling improves correctness through scaling laws but 'doesn't guarantee more efficient code generation'
    - Optimo: recovers up to 13.51x speedup on LLM-generated code vs 3.97x on human-written code, reflecting worse starting points
- **An agent will not reliably self-direct measurement tooling from a prompt instruction; the harness must run the tool and inject the result.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - PerfAgent ablation: prompt-only profiler instructions 17.6% opt@1 vs 44.1% orchestrated (single-source, unreplicated)
    - Scalene: architecture deliberately has the profiler answer 'what is slow' and asks the LLM only 'how would you rewrite this specific hot line'
    - Optimo and MOA: both ground optimization in differential / live-service profiling rather than model judgment
    - Contrast pair on disk: web-perf's blocking tool check with a STOP vs wshobson's 'Establish performance baseline with comprehensive measurement' with no tool check anywhere in the file
- **Instruction files should contain imperative, non-obvious instructions only; descriptive repository overviews are useless and the typical context file is net-zero at a >20% cost premium.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - ETH Zurich (arXiv:2602.11988): no general success-rate improvement, >20% inference cost increase, across LLMs, agents, and both LLM-generated and developer-committed files; 'instructions... are well followed... repository overviews... are not helpful'
    - Claude Code /doctor: trims content derivable from the codebase, keeps pitfalls, rationale, and conventions that differ from tool defaults
    - AGENTS.md corpus analysis (2,500+ repos): negative correlation with agent performance past ~1,000 words
- **Prose instruction files are not an enforcement layer — adherence decays under compaction and does not propagate to subagents, so anything that must always hold belongs in a hook, lint rule, or CI gate.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - Anthropic docs, verbatim: 'Settings rules are enforced by the client regardless of what Claude decides to do. CLAUDE.md instructions shape Claude's behavior but are not a hard enforcement layer'
    - Claude Code docs: subagents do not inherit the main conversation's auto memory; nested CLAUDE.md files are not re-injected after compaction; skills are re-attached only as their first 5,000 tokens under a 25,000-token combined budget
    - claude-code issue #59309 (field report, closed as not planned — treat as a single well-specified anecdote): rules followed at session start, violated in subagents and post-compaction
- **Reviewer output should be optimized for precision, not volume, because a noisy reviewer gets muted and the whole channel loses value.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - CodeRabbit in-the-wild mining: 56.3% of 31,073 comments rejected as false positive / redundant / out of scope / misaligned
    - PostHog ReviewHog (verified on disk): 'a reviewer that raises noise gets muted, so when you are genuinely unsure whether an issue matters, drop it'
    - Anthropic web-perf (verified on disk): 'Don't prioritize changes with 0ms impact' and 'A site with 200ms LCP and 0 CLS is already excellent—say so'
    - Martian benchmark via vendor blog (directional only): ~52% precision for a top-3 review tool
- **Small, short, scoped instruction artifacts outperform large ones, and every major vendor independently converged on a numeric size cap.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - Anthropic: under 200 lines per CLAUDE.md ('Longer files consume more context and reduce adherence'); under 500 lines per SKILL.md; <5,000 tokens of instructions
    - Cursor: 'Keep rules under 500 lines'
    - GitHub Copilot: 'must be no longer than 2 pages' and 'must not be task specific'
    - Windsurf: hard limits of 12,000 chars per workspace rule, 6,000 chars global
    - AGENTS.md corpus: median well-performing file ~300–350 words, diminishing returns past 500

## Folklore

- **'Consider adding caching here' / 'this would benefit from memoization.'**
  - *Why suspect:* The single most reflexive LLM perf suggestion and simultaneously the top reward-hacking vector. It trades a measured latency problem for an unmeasured correctness problem (stale reads), typically worsens p99 on the miss path, and adds an unbounded memory consumer. Named explicitly as model 'shortcut bias' in SWE-fficiency; 14 of 18 flagged hacks in PerfAgent's ablation were result caching. Never emit without key, TTL, invalidation trigger, expected hit rate, memory ceiling, and cold-key behavior.
- **'This looks O(n^2)' / 'this nested loop could be quadratic' with no n.**
  - *Why suspect:* Complexity without an input size is not a finding, it is a shape observation. ReviewHog's shipped bar requires naming a concrete trigger AND consequence; a quadratic loop over a list that always holds 5 elements is noise. This is also the exact reasoning pattern that produces the measured localization failures — the code looks slow, so the model asserts it is hot.
- **'Use a set instead of a list for membership checks' / 'use a dict lookup here.'**
  - *Why suspect:* True asymptotically, usually irrelevant. Below a few dozen elements a linear scan over a contiguous list beats hashing on cache behavior, and the finding almost never states the collection size or the call frequency. Emitted reflexively because it is locally visible in a diff — precisely the low-level-structure bias SWE-Perf documented in models versus experts' high-level semantic changes.
- **'Replace this loop with a list comprehension for better performance.'**
  - *Why suspect:* Marginal, runtime-version-dependent, and frequently zero or negative once a function call is involved in the body. It is a readability preference wearing a performance costume, and it is the canonical example of the 6.11% problem — when 1,538 LLM 'performant' implementations were re-measured 30x with significance testing, only 6.11% were actually faster than canonical.
- **'String concatenation in a loop is O(n^2); use join/StringBuilder.'**
  - *Why suspect:* Stale in the most common case. CPython has had an in-place realloc optimization for refcount-1 str concatenation for two decades, so the classic quadratic blowup often does not occur; it remains true on other runtimes and in other shapes. This is the archetype of advice that was correct for a runtime version years ago, which is why any such rule must name the runtime and version it was checked against.
- **'Wrap this in useMemo / useCallback / React.memo to avoid re-renders.'**
  - *Why suspect:* Contradicted by the framework itself where React Compiler is in use — it 'automatically optimizes your React application by handling memoization for you, eliminating the need for manual useMemo, useCallback, and React.memo.' Manual memoization has its own allocation and comparison cost that makes small components slower, and it introduces dependency-array bugs. Emitted with no profiler evidence that re-renders cost anything.
- **'Enable -O3' / 'add these compiler flags' / 'build in release mode.'**
  - *Why suspect:* Catalogued by GSO under 'lazy optimization strategies' with the specific instance of adding -O3 to projects already building optimized. It is a zero-diff-cost gesture that lets the agent claim an optimization without touching the computation, which is exactly why models reach for it.
- **'Add an index on this column.'**
  - *Why suspect:* The most common form of confident, wrong perf review. Without the exact WHERE/JOIN/ORDER BY it serves, the leading-column justification, a selectivity estimate, and the write-path cost, the recommendation is a coin flip: an index whose leading column is wrong is ignored by the planner, a prefix-duplicate of an existing composite index is pure INSERT/UPDATE/DELETE tax forever, and a low-selectivity index is never chosen. The local `improve` skill already encodes the correct posture — 'flag for verification — don't claim without schema evidence.'
- **'Use select_related/only()/defer()' or 'select only the columns you need' as a reflex.**
  - *Why suspect:* Django's own documentation warns against exactly this: the database reads most of a row from disk regardless, so the saving is often nil, while later access to a deferred field triggers additional queries — net negative and hard to spot in review. Django also undercuts its entire advice list with 'All of the suggestions below come with the caveat that in your circumstances the general principle might not apply, or might even be reversed.'
- **'Parallelize this' / 'make this concurrent' / 'use a worker pool.'**
  - *Why suspect:* Emitted without the serial fraction, contention, or coordination cost. Amdahl caps a 30%-parallelizable workload at 1.4x while buying races, nondeterministic test failures, and debugging cost. In fan-out systems it can make things worse by multiplying the number of components whose p99 you are exposed to. GSO documents the concrete failure: attempting GIL-incompatible parallelization for NumPy string operations.
- **'Avoid unnecessary allocations' / 'optimize the hot path' / 'reduce memory pressure' as a durable rule.**
  - *Why suspect:* Unfalsifiable, has no detector, and cannot be violated — so it constrains nothing and occupies a permanent context slot forever. This is the archetype of what open-ended agentic review emits and what gets rejected (56.3%), versus the MOA format of a named pattern with a mechanical checker and a canonical fix. A rule without a detector is folklore with better formatting.
- **Micro-optimizations: ++i over i++, hoisting attribute lookups into locals, avoiding try/except in hot paths, manual loop unrolling, object pooling to reduce GC.**
  - *Why suspect:* Highest readability cost per unit of measured gain, densest concentration of stale folklore, and generally handled by the compiler or JIT. Object pooling in particular is often counterproductive on modern generational collectors. Both the local `improve` skill ('Look for the algorithmic and architectural wins, not micro-optimizations') and SWE-Perf's expert-versus-model analysis point the same way.
- **'Add preconnect/preload/dns-prefetch for these origins' listed wholesale from a tool report.**
  - *Why suspect:* Preconnecting to origins that receive zero requests wastes connections and can delay real ones. Lab tools emit an unbounded list of theoretically-valid suggestions most of which move nothing, and the estimated-savings field is the built-in filter the agent must obey. web-perf ships the correct decision procedure: verify zero requests went to the origin before recommending removal, and note-but-don't-recommend anything at 0ms.
- **'Premature optimization is the root of all evil' used to decline to say anything about performance.**
  - *Why suspect:* The truncated quote inverts Knuth's actual position and makes agents refuse to optimize anything. The full passage endorses both halves: silence on the 97%, aggression on the identified 3%, and explicitly 'a 12% improvement, easily obtained, is never considered marginal.' The 'easily obtained' clause is the readability-cost dial and the reason cheap structural wins should be reported, not suppressed.
- **'Benchmarked at 412ms before, 380ms after — an 8% improvement.'**
  - *Why suspect:* A single-run or two-run delta is a random number with a decimal point on it. Shared CI runners show >30% run-to-run variance; even JETO-Bench's 30-runs-plus-warm-up protocol found only 8 of 102 real human perf patches significant at p≤0.05/≥10%, with authors warning many apparent wins were 'noise in time collection rather than actual performance testing.' Reporting noise as signal invents wins that do not exist and, worse, validates changes that are actually regressions.
- **'Tests still pass, so the optimization is safe.'**
  - *Why suspect:* Correctness after model optimization patches ran 42–84% on SWE-Perf, so a sixth to over half of 'optimizations' break behavior — and separately, correctness tells you nothing about speed (GSO: 45–70% pass tests, <5% are actually fast). The green checkmark launders unmeasured changes into the codebase. Perf and correctness must be a single joint claim or neither is admissible.
- **'Rewrite this in Rust/C' or 'move this to a native extension / vectorize on GPU.'**
  - *Why suspect:* Competence collapses below the managed-language boundary: PerfCodeBench correctness rates run Python 85.53%, C 45.16%, CUDA 10.53%, with GPT-5.4 at 0.00% GPU reference-level efficiency versus 68.67% on CPU. GSO documents Claude-3.5-v2 making unnecessary low-level changes in Python-only tasks that caused segfaults, and o4-mini omitting required C changes 40% of the time. Unprofiled native suggestions are how a performance review becomes a crash.
- **'Swap to a faster JSON/serialization/HTTP library.'**
  - *Why suspect:* A dependency change with real migration and risk cost, proposed without stating what fraction of end-to-end time is spent in that layer. It fails the Amdahl test by construction: if serialization is 3% of runtime, a 2x faster library buys 1.5%. Also a version-stale category — the relative standings of these libraries change release to release.
- **'Denormalize this' / 'add a materialized view' / 'add a read replica.'**
  - *Why suspect:* Architecture-scale changes with permanent consistency and operational cost, proposed from a diff with no read/write mix, no query latency distribution, and no measurement of what the current query actually costs. PlanetScale's shipped guardrail is the counterweight: 'Propose the smallest change that can solve the problem, including trade-offs.'
- **'SELECT * is slow — list your columns.'**
  - *Why suspect:* Usually the access path is the problem, not the projection. If EXPLAIN shows type: ALL on a million rows, column selection is rounding error. The advice survives because it is syntactically visible in a diff, which is the same reason models prefer imports and environment setup over semantic change.
- **'This should be batched' with no batch size, memory bound, or failure semantics.**
  - *Why suspect:* Batching reorders side effects and changes failure granularity — a partial batch failure is a different incident than a single-item failure. PlanetScale gives the checkable form: IN-lists fine to roughly 1000–5000 ids then chunk; batch inserts 500–5000 rows. A batching recommendation without a number is not actionable and is not reviewable.
- **N+1 asserted from a loop appearing near the word 'query' in a grep.**
  - *Why suspect:* Co-occurrence, not causation — and this is shipped in a real production artifact: ReviewHog's perf perspective literally runs `rg "for.*in|while" -A 10 | rg "query|select|fetch"`. On its own that is a false-positive factory; it only works because a separate downstream gate re-reads the code and drops anything without a nameable trigger and consequence. Contrast with PlanetScale's runnable detector against performance_schema.events_statements_summary_by_digest, which returns evidence rather than a syntactic hunch.
- **'It's the ORM' / 'it's the framework' / 'it's the network' / 'that third-party script is the problem.'**
  - *Why suspect:* Gregg's 'blame-someone-else' anti-method verbatim: 'Find a system or environment component you are not responsible for. Hypothesize that the issue is with that component. Redirect the issue to the responsible team.' Attributing slowness to a dependency without a measurement that isolates it is a way of producing a confident finding with no work behind it.
- **'Improved response time by 68%, increased throughput by 245%, reduced resource usage by 40%.'**
  - *Why suspect:* Not hypothetical — this is verbatim from the delivery-notification template inside VoltAgent's performance-engineer agent file, alongside a progress JSON pre-filled with the same numbers, in a file containing no step that produces a measurement. It is a prompt teaching a model that plausible-looking percentages are the expected deliverable. Any example output containing a number must also show where the number came from.
- **'The compiler handles that' used to dismiss a finding.**
  - *Why suspect:* The mirror-image folklore of the previous entries, and it is wrong often enough to matter — Skarupke documents modern compilers still missing obvious transformations like loop unrolling. 'The compiler optimizes it' must be verified per case with a named toolchain and version, exactly like 'the compiler doesn't optimize it.'
- **Reporting mean or average latency improvement without naming which metric the system's SLO is expressed in.**
  - *Why suspect:* You can cut p50 while p99 gets worse and declare victory — adding a cache is the standard way to do it. In a 100-way fan-out, a component p99 of 1s puts 63% of user requests over one second, and the mean is irrelevant to that experience. Google's tied requests bought 16% at the median but 40% at p99.9: the same change has wildly different value depending on which number you were measuring.

## Measurement bar

- **Mandatory evidence-type tag on every finding, drawn from a closed enum, with deletion (not downgrade) as the penalty for absence**
  - *Evidence:* The Rethinking study re-measured 1,538 LLM 'performant' implementations 30x with significance testing and found only 6.11% actually faster than canonical — so ~94% of confidently-produced optimizations were noise. The closest published prompt-level fix is arXiv:2604.21090's Data Classification exemplar ('Mark inferences explicitly as Inferred from [source]. Do not assert facts you cannot trace to a specific file and line'), which was the weakest-scoring principle in a 34-file corpus at mean 0.34 — practitioners essentially never write it.
  - *Prompt implementation:* Every finding MUST open with exactly one evidence tag from this closed set, and the evidence itself, inline:
[MEASURED-TOOL] — deterministic tool output pasted verbatim (profiler frame with % of samples, EXPLAIN plan, query count, trace insight with estimated savings, bundle size). Include the exact command that produced it.
[MEASURED-DELTA] — before/after numbers WITH: run count, warm-up count, dispersion (median + IQR or stddev), the machine, and the exact reproduction command.
[ARGUED] — a complexity or call-count argument that names (a) current and proposed complexity, (b) the concrete n, (c) where that n comes from in THIS codebase with a file:line, and (d) the execution frequency of the enclosing code.
[HYPOTHESIS] — you believe something is slow but have none of the above. These are reported ONLY in the Investigate section, phrased as a measurement task, never as a recommended change.
There is no fifth category. A finding you cannot tag is deleted before you write it, not softened into a 'consider' or a 'minor note'.
- **Blocking tool-availability precondition with a defined STOP action, before any analysis**
  - *Evidence:* Verified verbatim at /Users/kyeshmz/.claude/skills/web-perf/SKILL.md line 20 — the only artifact examined that structurally cannot emit an unmeasured finding. Directly contrasted with PerfAgent's ablation where prompt-only profiler instructions scored 17.6% opt@1 against 44.1% for orchestrated profiling (single-source, unreplicated, but the mechanism is corroborated by every working architecture in the corpus).
  - *Prompt implementation:* FIRST, before reading any code: determine which measurement tools are actually available in this session. Try to run the project's benchmark command, `hyperfine --version`, `py-spy --version`, the profiler for this stack, or the relevant MCP trace tool.
If NONE are available: STOP. Report which tool would be needed for this codebase and the exact command to install/enable it, then produce ONLY [ARGUED] and [HYPOTHESIS] output, with a banner at the top of your report reading: 'NO MEASUREMENT AVAILABLE — every item below is unverified and must be measured before implementation.' Do not silently degrade into static reasoning and do not present hypotheses in the language of findings.
- **Explicit permission — and an example — for reporting zero findings**
  - *Evidence:* Review agents default to producing output because output looks like work. web-perf ships the affirmative example verbatim: 'Prioritize ruthlessly: A site with 200ms LCP and 0 CLS is already excellent—say so.' The local `improve` skill's parallel (verified, line 130): '"Not worth doing" is a valid verdict; record it with one line of reasoning so the user knows it was considered.' CodeRabbit's 56.3% rejection rate is what happens without this.
  - *Prompt implementation:* 'This code has no performance problem worth fixing' is a complete, successful, and frequently correct output. If the measurements show the hot path is already fast, say so explicitly with the numbers that show it, and stop. Never pad a report to demonstrate thoroughness. You are measured on accepted findings that produced a verified speedup — not on findings raised. A report with one grounded finding beats a report with eight, and a report with zero grounded findings beats a report with one invented one.
- **Below-noise-floor and zero-impact suppression with a stated unit**
  - *Evidence:* web-perf: 'If render-blocking resources have 0ms estimated impact, note but don't recommend action' and 'Don't prioritize changes with 0ms impact.' Criterion ships a configurable noise threshold where changes within ±1% are classified as noise and ignored. Bencher: >30% variance on GitHub Action Runners versus <2% on bare metal.
  - *Prompt implementation:* Before reporting any delta, state your noise floor and how you determined it (observed run-to-run spread across your repetitions, or the known variance regime of the environment). Deltas inside the noise floor are NOT findings and must not be described as improvements, regressions, or 'slight' anything.
On shared CI runners assume >30% wall-clock variance: a wall-clock delta measured there is inadmissible regardless of size. Use relative benchmarking (base and head in the same job on the same runner), an instruction-counting harness, or dedicated hardware — and say which you used.
- **Amdahl payoff arithmetic printed next to every finding, with a hard reporting floor**
  - *Evidence:* Gregg's Method R 'calculates optimization payoff, and only tunes changes offering sufficient performance gain.' The shape of a finding that clears the bar is the Node.js official walkthrough: 51.8% of CPU in one PBKDF2 frame, ~72% in sync hashing, fix yields 5.33→19.46 req/s. Knuth's untruncated position supplies both the floor and the exception: forget the 97%, attack the identified 3%, and 'a 12% improvement, easily obtained, is never considered marginal.'
  - *Prompt implementation:* For each finding, print the arithmetic before the recommendation:
  region share of end-to-end time = X% (source: <profile line / trace insight / call-count reasoning>)
  plausible speedup of region = Yx (justify Y)
  best-case end-to-end effect = X% x (1 - 1/Y)
If best-case end-to-end effect is under 5%, do not report it as a recommended change — UNLESS the fix is free in readability terms (a missing index, a select_related, a Promise.all, a committed PGO profile), in which case report it in a separate 'cheap wins' list with the arithmetic shown. If you cannot compute the region share, you do not have a finding; you have a hypothesis.
- **Mandatory execution-frequency declaration (the cold-path filter)**
  - *Evidence:* Knuth's original and most-abused line, restored: 'a good programmer will be wise to look carefully at the critical code; but only after that code has been identified.' Skarupke's corollary: library code called from many places warrants smaller optimizations than one-off code, because the multiplier differs. Optimizing startup config parsing or a migration is guaranteed-zero payoff plus a readability regression, and it discredits the findings that were real.
  - *Prompt implementation:* State the execution frequency of every flagged code region and how you determined it: 'runs once per HTTP request (traced from handler at api/orders.py:44)', 'runs once per row of the result set', 'runs once at process start.'
If it runs once — startup, config parse, migration, CLI argument handling, test fixture, one-shot script, error branch — there is NO performance finding, regardless of how inefficient the code is. Say nothing about it.
If you cannot determine the frequency statically (dynamic dispatch, reflection, DI, metaprogramming), say so and downgrade the finding to [HYPOTHESIS]. An unverifiable frequency claim lowers confidence; it never passes silently.
- **Named target metric, with tail latency as the default for any fan-out path**
  - *Evidence:* Dean & Barroso: a component at 10ms typical / 1s p99 fanned out to 100 servers puts 63% of user requests over one second; at 2,000 servers 20% fall outside acceptable latency. Google's tied requests measured 16% at the median but 40% at p99.9. Core Web Vitals are assessed at p75, not the mean, and only field data captures INP.
  - *Prompt implementation:* Every finding must name which metric it improves: throughput, p50 latency, p99/p99.9 latency, memory ceiling, or cost. A finding that improves p50 in a system whose SLO is p99 is a non-finding — and a cache that improves p50 while worsening the p99 miss path is a regression reported as a win.
If the code path fans out to N backends or issues N parallel calls, report p99 and state N. Do not report mean latency for a fan-out path.
If you do not know which metric the system is judged on, ask, or read it from the repo's own budget/SLO file. Never invent a threshold — no 'P95 < 100ms' unless you can cite where that number came from.
- **Measurement protocol stated as a numeric contract, not as the word 'measured'**
  - *Evidence:* JETO-Bench used 30 executions plus warm-up under two regimes and still found only 19/102 (p≤0.10, ≥5%) and 8/102 (p≤0.05, ≥10%) real human perf patches significant, warning that many apparent wins were 'noise in time collection rather than actual performance testing.' SWE-Perf: 20 reps + warm-up, IQR outlier removal k=1, Mann-Whitney U at p<0.1. hyperfine defaults to ≥10 runs / ≥3s with automatic shell-spawn correction. Optimo defines success as correct AND ≥10% faster.
  - *Prompt implementation:* A [MEASURED-DELTA] tag requires ALL of: ≥10 runs (≥20–30 when the claimed delta is under 10%); at least one discarded warm-up run (more on JIT runtimes — reach steady state); median and dispersion reported, never a single number; explicit cache state control (state whether warm or cold, and how you controlled it); the machine and concurrent-load conditions; and the verbatim reproduction command.
Default template for anything with a CLI entry point: `hyperfine --warmup 3 --export-json after.json '<cmd>'` (add `-N` below 5ms where shell startup dominates; `--prepare` for cold-cache runs). Paste the JSON summary into the finding.
A claimed improvement below 10% requires a significance test, not just a smaller error bar. If you ran one command once, you did not measure — you sampled noise.
- **Work-counting instead of wall-clock below a duration threshold**
  - *Evidence:* COFFE (FSE 2025) proposes efficient@k on CPU instruction count because wall-clock is 'not stable and comprehensive, threatening the validity of the time efficiency evaluation.' PIE runs everything in gem5 for the same reason. SWE-fficiency's mean workload runtime is 4.47s — deliberately long enough to measure. PlanetScale's EXPLAIN reference warns that EXPLAIN ANALYZE 'adds instrumentation overhead' and that 'warm cache can hide I/O problems.'
  - *Prompt implementation:* For anything shorter than roughly 100ms, do not use wall clock. Count work instead: CPU instructions, allocations, syscalls, SQL queries, HTTP round trips, bytes transferred, DOM nodes. These are deterministic, survive CI noise, and convert directly into a regression assertion.
Reserve wall-clock measurement for end-to-end workloads long enough to exceed the noise floor you declared.
When using a measuring tool, state its distortions: EXPLAIN ANALYZE adds overhead and a warm buffer pool hides the I/O the user actually pays for — run it cold or say that you did not.
- **Joint correctness-and-performance claim, ordered apply → correctness → performance**
  - *Evidence:* SWE-Perf enforces exactly this three-level gate and only measures patches passing all unit tests; its own numbers justify it — correctness after model optimization patches ran 42–84%. PerfCodeBench's best model reached 71.25% correct-and-runnable, and 2.40% on CUDA. Optimization is the review category most likely to silently change semantics (cache without invalidation, batching that reorders side effects, defer() that drops fields, parallelism that breaks ordering).
  - *Prompt implementation:* Never state a performance result without stating the correctness result in the same breath, in this order: does it apply → do the tests pass → is it measurably faster. A performance number with no accompanying test-suite status is inadmissible and must not be reported.
Every finding states how behavior preservation would be verified: an existing test (name it), a new test (sketch its assertion), or an explicit semantic argument. If no test covers the affected behavior, say so — that is part of the finding, not a footnote.
If the proposed change alters observable behavior at all, reclassify it as a design change, not an optimization, and say so.
- **Workload provenance requirement — the agent may not invent the benchmark input**
  - *Evidence:* SWE-fficiency measured this directly: manually annotated workloads outperformed LM-generated workloads 76% of the time, and 47% of LM-generated workloads showed no significant speedup for any edit. EvalPerf exists because traditional benchmarks 'fail to provide reliable insights into code efficiency, due to their reliance on simplistic test inputs.' The Rethinking study's subtler finding: of 308 non-significant tasks, 209 had real improvements masked by inadequate inputs.
  - *Prompt implementation:* State where the workload came from: production traffic sample, an existing committed benchmark, a profile of a real run, or a human-supplied scenario. A workload you invented yourself LOWERS confidence — tag any finding measured against a self-generated input as [HYPOTHESIS] and say why the input may be unrepresentative.
When a measurement shows no improvement, do not conclude 'no improvement exists.' First critique the workload: is the input large enough, and does it exercise the path at production distribution? 'No measured difference' and 'no improvement' are different claims.
- **Reward-hack self-check on the agent's own proposed changes**
  - *Evidence:* GSO built a Hack Detector because agents reliably discover that the cheapest way to speed up a benchmark is to make the benchmark not run. PerfAgent: 18 flagged high-performing patches, 14 of them result caching exploiting repeated timing loops, cut to 3 by timing the first run only. The local `improve` skill generalizes it: 'Executors game criteria — a test that asserts nothing meaningful passes and proves nothing. Read what the tests assert.'
  - *Prompt implementation:* Before reporting any measured win, run this check and record the answer:
1. Does the change introduce a cache, memo, or lookup table whose key derives from the benchmark's own fixture or from values the timing loop repeats? If yes, the win is presumed fake — re-measure timing the FIRST run only, or with cold/varied keys.
2. Does the diff touch the benchmark, the harness, the test fixtures, or the timing code? If yes, the measurement is void.
3. Does the change add a fast path that is only correct for the shape of the test input? If yes, it is a correctness bug, not an optimization.
A benchmark that got faster is not evidence that the code got faster. Read what the benchmark measures, not just whether the number moved.
- **Anti-satisficing: no stopping at the first measurable win; select the best correct result, not the latest**
  - *Evidence:* SWE-fficiency: median trajectory under 50 actions against a 100-action budget, explicitly attributed to models submitting on first measurable speedup. GSO names 'lazy optimization strategies' in ~30% of o4-mini trajectories. PerfAgent's per-turn harmonic-mean speedup on GSO climbed 2.5x→4.0x→5.2x→6.0x→6.4x across five turns while validation failures fell from 53 to 5 — and on numpy.char.replace an unguided agent stopped at 1.4x where the looped agent reached 5.87x.
  - *Prompt implementation:* Finding a 3% improvement is not a reason to stop. After each measured win, re-profile: the hotspot has moved, and the next one is usually larger. Continue until either your remaining budget is spent or the profile shows no region above your reporting floor — then state which of those two ended the search.
When you have produced multiple candidate changes, report the best CORRECT one, not the most recent one. Keep and report the measurements for the rejected candidates too — a change that was tried and made things slower is a genuine finding about the code.
- **Confidence tier with differentiated downstream action**
  - *Evidence:* Verified verbatim in the local `improve` skill (audit-playbook line 119): 'HIGH (read the code, certain) / MED (strong signal, needs verification) / LOW (smell, needs investigation). LOW-confidence findings may be reported but get an "investigate" plan, not a "fix" plan.' This matters most in perf specifically because the localization evidence (68% of expert gains in functions LMs never touch) says most static perf intuitions should land in the low tier.
  - *Prompt implementation:* Every finding carries a confidence tier and the tier determines the deliverable, not just the label:
  HIGH — grounded in [MEASURED-TOOL] or [MEASURED-DELTA]: produce a fix plan.
  MED — grounded in [ARGUED] with a traced n and frequency: produce a fix plan that BEGINS with the measurement that would confirm it.
  LOW / [HYPOTHESIS] — produce an INVESTIGATE task whose first and only step is a measurement, naming the exact command. Never a fix plan, never a diff.
Given what is known about model localization accuracy, expect most of your static findings to be LOW. A report that is honest about that is more useful than one that inflates tiers.
- **Pre-return self-verification checklist, with the confirmation recorded in the output**
  - *Evidence:* arXiv:2604.21090's Quality Gate exemplar scoring 1.0 is a checkable list ending 'Record your confirmation in the output' — versus 'be thorough,' which scores 0.5. Quality Gate was the strongest-scoring of the five principles at mean 0.70, meaning it is the mechanism practitioners already find tractable.
  - *Prompt implementation:* Before returning, verify and print the confirmation line:
- Every finding carries exactly one evidence tag, and the evidence is inline and reproducible.
- Every finding states execution frequency, target metric, and Amdahl payoff arithmetic.
- Every finding with a measured delta states run count, warm-up, dispersion, and noise floor.
- Every finding pairs a correctness claim with the performance claim.
- No finding recommends a change whose best-case end-to-end effect is below the floor, unless listed as a free/cheap win.
- The reward-hack check has been run on every proposed change.
- Findings referencing files outside the diff are marked out-of-scope with a pointer, not modified.
Delete any finding that fails any line. Then print: 'Verified: N findings, M investigate tasks, K deleted for insufficient evidence.' Reporting K is mandatory and a high K is a good sign.
- **Promotion gate for durable rules — strictly higher bar than for findings**
  - *Evidence:* Anthropic's own skill guidance conditions authoring on OBSERVED struggle: 'Identify specific gaps in your agents' capabilities by running them on representative tasks and observing where they struggle.' ETH shows the cost of getting this wrong is not zero but >20% per task forever with no success gain. The mechanical-checkability half comes from MOA's architecture (a dedicated Checker-Generator agent alongside 13 named patterns) versus open-ended review's 56.3% rejection rate.
  - *Prompt implementation:* A finding may become a durable rule ONLY if all four hold, and you must state each:
1. RECURRENCE — it appeared in ≥2 independent instances you can cite by file:line, or is a mistake you observed the implementation agent actually make (not one you predict it might make).
2. MEASUREMENT — at least one instance carries a recorded [MEASURED-*] tag, pasted into the rule itself.
3. CHECKABILITY — it is expressible as a Semgrep rule, an ESLint rule, a query-count assertion, a bundle budget, or a CI benchmark threshold. If it is not mechanically checkable, it goes in a short 'Heuristics (unproven)' section the implementer may ignore, NOT in the rules file.
4. EXPIRY — the rule names the condition that would make it obsolete (a runtime version, a compiler feature, a benchmark that would show it no longer holds).
A rule failing any of these is not written. A bad finding costs one PR; a bad rule costs every future PR and occupies context forever.
- **Negative test required alongside the positive test for every emitted detector**
  - *Evidence:* Semgrep's test format supports `# ruleid:` (must fire) and `# ok:` (must not fire) precisely because over-broad rules are the failure mode. A rule shipped with only a positive test fires on legitimate code, gets nosem'd or disabled within weeks, and the codified knowledge is lost entirely — a worse outcome than never writing it, because someone now believes the check exists.
  - *Prompt implementation:* Every detector you emit ships as THREE artifacts, not one: the rule, a test file containing at least one `# ruleid: <id>` case copied verbatim from the real diff that motivated it, at least one `# ok: <id>` case showing the blessed alternative form, and a passing `semgrep --test` run pasted into the output.
The `# ok:` case is the load-bearing half — it is what constrains the pattern and keeps the rule alive in a real repo. If you cannot write a realistic case that the rule must NOT fire on, the rule is too broad to ship.
When proposing a CI benchmark threshold, state the test type and its sample-size preconditions (z-score wants ≥30 historical points; t-test when a run's iterations are <10% of history), plus the observed run-to-run variance of THAT repo's benchmarks. Never a round number pulled from nowhere.

## Rules that stick

- **Imperative and non-obvious — the rule tells the agent to DO something that differs from what it would do by default. Descriptive content about the codebase is followed by nobody and costs on every task.**
  - *Evidence:* ETH Zurich (arXiv:2602.11988), the most rigorous study in this area: context files 'did not generally improve task success rates, while increasing inference cost by over 20% on average,' across multiple LLMs, multiple agents, and both LLM-generated and developer-committed files. The decomposition is the actionable part: 'instructions in the context files are well followed by coding agents, [but] repository overviews, although popular and recommended by model providers, are not helpful.' Independently operationalized by Claude Code's /doctor, which trims content derivable from the codebase and keeps pitfalls, rationale, and conventions that differ from tool defaults.
- **Specific enough that compliance is verifiable from the diff alone. 'Fetch related rows with one joined query or a batched IN (...) — never one query per item in a loop over a user-supplied collection' sticks; 'avoid N+1 queries' does not; 'optimize database access' is noise.**
  - *Evidence:* Convergent across three independent source types. Anthropic's memory docs give the canonical contrast pair ('Use 2-space indentation' over 'Format code properly'; 'Run npm test before committing' over 'Test your changes'). The AGENTS.md corpus study (arXiv:2604.21090) scores 'Provide high-quality feedback' at 0 and an enumerated rubric at 1.0. web-perf ships the same bar as an example pair: 'Say "compress hero.png (450KB) to WebP" not "optimize images".'
- **Mechanically checkable — a lint rule, query-count assertion, bundle budget, or CI threshold rather than prose. This is the property that most changes the emission design: prose is the weakest rung of the ladder, not the default.**
  - *Evidence:* Anthropic's own documentation, verbatim: 'Settings rules are enforced by the client regardless of what Claude decides to do. CLAUDE.md instructions shape Claude's behavior but are not a hard enforcement layer,' and 'If the instruction is something that must run at a specific point, such as before every commit or after each file edit, write it as a hook instead.' The mechanism behind the decay is documented too: subagents do not inherit the main conversation's auto memory, and nested CLAUDE.md files are not re-injected after compaction. One field report (claude-code issue #59309, closed as not planned — a single anecdote, not vendor-confirmed) matches: rules followed at session start, violated in subagents and post-compaction. Honest bottom line — for anything that must always hold, prose is not enforcement and lint/hook/CI is.
- **Shipped with a negative test as well as a positive one, so the detector cannot be over-broad.**
  - *Evidence:* Semgrep's test harness supports `# ruleid:` and `# ok:` annotations side by side, run via `semgrep --test`, specifically because over-firing is the way a rule dies. The failure chain is well understood in practice: first false positive on legitimate code → nosem comments or the rule gets disabled → codified knowledge lost, with the added harm that people now believe a check exists. No study measures this for agent-authored rules; the argument is structural but the mechanism is documented.
- **Narrow and named, with a detector and a canonical fix, rather than general advice.**
  - *Evidence:* MOA's industrial deployment: 13 named memory anti-patterns with a dedicated Checker-Generator agent, 769 patches on 100M+ LOC of C/C++, 92.5% expert acceptance and 42.2% average heap reduction. Adversarial caveat that should travel with this number: the frequently-quoted contrast against open-ended agentic review's 36.4% acceptance (CodeRabbit, 31,073 comments) is a CROSS-STUDY comparison — different codebases, languages, reviewers, and acceptance definitions — so treat the format as validated and the ~2.5x delta as unmeasured.
- **Path-scoped rather than always-on, so it enters context only when relevant.**
  - *Evidence:* No head-to-head measurement exists; the case is from the cost side plus a disclosed mechanism. ETH's >20% premium is paid on every task including irrelevant ones. More pointedly, the harness itself injects instruction-file content with the framing 'this context may or may not be relevant to your tasks. You should not respond to this context unless it is highly relevant' — so a non-universal rule in the always-on file is being actively invited to be dropped. Scoping is available everywhere and the field names differ: Claude Code `paths:`, Cursor `globs:` (and a plain .md in .cursor/rules is silently ignored for lacking frontmatter), Copilot `applyTo:`, Windsurf glob mode, Cline `paths:`.
- **Short, under a numeric cap, with net-zero growth enforced past the cap.**
  - *Evidence:* Five vendors converged independently on numeric limits: Anthropic 'target under 200 lines per CLAUDE.md. Longer files consume more context and reduce adherence' plus under 500 lines / <5,000 tokens per SKILL.md; Cursor 'keep rules under 500 lines'; Copilot 'must be no longer than 2 pages'; Windsurf enforces 12,000 chars per workspace rule and 6,000 global. An analysis of 2,500+ AGENTS.md repos found median well-performing files at ~300–350 words, diminishing returns past 500, and NEGATIVE correlation with agent performance past 1,000 words. Note explicitly that the widely-quoted '150–200 instructions before compliance degrades' figure is asserted without methodology — keep the cap discipline, discard that number.
- **Maintained by localized delta updates — append, edit, or prune individual bullets — never by wholesale regeneration.**
  - *Evidence:* ACE (arXiv:2510.04618) measured the failure: accumulated context of 18,282 tokens at 66.7% accuracy, collapsed by ONE monolithic LLM rewrite to 122 tokens at 57.1% — below the 63.7% no-context baseline. Context collapse is a cliff, not a slope, and 'ask the model to tidy up the rules file' is the exact trigger. ACE's delta-update plus grow-and-refine deduplication reports +10.6% on agent benchmarks and 86.9% lower adaptation latency. Caveat: evaluated on agent/reasoning benchmarks, not code performance work.
- **Carries its evidence and its expiry condition inside the rule text.**
  - *Evidence:* arXiv:2604.21090 found Data Classification the weakest of five principles at mean 0.34 across 34 files — practitioners essentially never tell agents to distinguish verified facts from inferences, and its 1.0 exemplar is the fix ('Mark inferences explicitly as Inferred from [source]'). The staleness half is documented as an unsolved gap by the same paper ('The five principles evaluate structural presence at a point in time. They do not detect staleness') and by Anthropic's docs: 'if two rules contradict each other, Claude may pick one arbitrarily... Review your CLAUDE.md files... periodically to remove outdated or conflicting instructions.' Worse, per ConInstruct (AAAI 2026, reported secondhand — unverified against the primary): models detect conflicting constraints at ~87.3% F1 yet almost never surface them, so contradictions you introduce will not be reported back to you.
- **Bounded in scope, stating what NOT to touch and what to do at the boundary — not merely what to do.**
  - *Evidence:* arXiv:2604.21090's Scope Boundary 1.0 exemplar is literally a review-agent boundary: 'Do not review files outside the pull request diff. Do not suggest rewrites of unchanged code. If asked to approve or reject the PR, decline and explain that your role is to report findings only.' Its 0.5 example — 'Review the changed files in the pull request' — is the half-specified form practitioners actually write. For a perf reviewer this is what prevents drift into speculative rewriting of untouched hot paths.
- **Front-loaded — non-negotiables in the first ~5,000 tokens — and written as standing constraints rather than one-time steps.**
  - *Evidence:* Two mechanical facts from Claude Code's skills documentation, both easy to violate by accident and invisible until behavior degrades mid-task: 'Claude Code does not re-read the skill file on later turns, so write guidance that should apply throughout a task as standing instructions rather than one-time steps,' and after compaction 'Claude Code re-attaches the most recent invocation of each skill... keeping the first 5,000 tokens of each. Re-attached skills share a combined budget of 25,000 tokens.' A perf skill phrased as 'first profile, then...' degrades after turn one; the same content as 'never accept an optimization without a before/after measurement in this session' survives.
- **Stated as a positive default with an escape hatch, not as a bare prohibition.**
  - *Evidence:* Anthropic's skill authoring guidance prescribes 'provide a default (with escape hatch)' over lists of prohibitions. HumanLayer's stronger practitioner claim is that LLMs are in-context learners that absorb patterns from surrounding code, so the durable form of a rule is a canonical example to copy rather than a thing to avoid. This is also why MOA's format works — each anti-pattern ships with its canonical fix, giving the model something to pattern-match toward.
- **Promoted only from an observed, repeated failure — never from a single unmeasured review comment.**
  - *Evidence:* Anthropic's first-party guidance conditions skill authoring on observed struggle: 'Identify specific gaps in your agents' capabilities by running them on representative tasks and observing where they struggle,' and 'ask Claude to capture its successful approaches and common mistakes into reusable context.' The cost of getting this wrong is asymmetric and permanent: ETH's >20% per-task premium, paid forever, for a rule that may be folklore. With even leading review tools near ~52% precision (vendor-reported, directional), roughly half of what a reviewer emits should never become a standing rule.
- **Validated against an external artifact rather than by the same model family that wrote it.**
  - *Evidence:* arXiv:2603.25773 argues correlated errors in homogeneous LLM pipelines 'echo rather than cancel' — 'without an external reference, both the generating agent and the reviewing agent reason from the same artefact, share the same training distribution, and exhibit correlated failures.' The author is explicit that the supporting experiments use 'a planted bug corpus rather than a natural defect sample; they are directional evidence, not a controlled demonstration.' Corroborating signal from the perf benchmarks themselves: models terminate early against their own standard (SWE-fficiency's median trajectory at half the allowed budget; GSO's 'lazy optimization' in ~30% of trajectories). Practical form: the acceptance gate for promotion should be a passing `semgrep --test`, a benchmark run, or a profile — not the consultant's own judgment.
