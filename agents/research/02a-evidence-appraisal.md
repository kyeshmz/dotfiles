# Adversarial Source Appraisal — What Is Actually Supported

An appraiser agent scored every distinct approach found in sweep 2 as battle-tested / plausible-unproven / popular-but-unsupported / contradicted-by-evidence, with an adopt-adapt-reject call. The **Cargo cult** section is the highest-value part: widely repeated planner-agent advice that the evidence does not support, or actively contradicts.

## Verdicts

- **No planner agent at all — a fixed, human-authored localize→repair→validate pipeline (Agentless), with the same-model comparison from Kimi-Dev**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* The strongest evidence in the corpus that a model-authored plan is not where the points come from. Agentless: 32.00% SWE-bench Lite at $0.70/issue vs SWE-agent $1.62-2.53 and CodeR $3.34, with a clean per-stage ablation. Kimi-Dev is the decisive datum because it holds the model constant: 60.4% Verified on the fixed Agentless workflow vs 48.6% pass@1 driving a free-form SWE-Agent — an 11.8 pt penalty for letting the model choose its own control flow. Two independent groups. Honest caveats: Agentless's absolute number is 2024 and has been passed by free-form agents since (mini-SWE-agent >74%), so the claim is 'at a given model capability, an authored pipeline beats a model-invented one at a fraction of cost', not 'agents lose'. And SWE-ABS (arXiv 2603.00520) shows SWE-bench-style scores are inflated by weak test coverage, so treat all these absolutes as ordinal, not cardinal.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Planner SELECTS from a small set of pre-written plan templates rather than authoring one from a blank page (CodeR task graphs)**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* CodeR hit 28.33% SWE-bench Lite (+10.33 over SWE-agent at the time); removing multi-agent+task-graphs dropped its 50-issue ablation subset from 22% to 10%. The authors' stated reason is the transferable part: on-the-go control-flow decisions rely on instruction-following and long-horizon memory, which the PlanBench work independently shows LLMs are bad at. Converges with Kimi-Dev and Agentless on the same mechanism. Weakness: the ablation n=50, and the templates were tuned for SWE-bench's issue-resolution shape specifically. Adapt rather than adopt because a template menu only helps if the menu actually covers your task distribution; a mis-fitting template is exactly the 'bad plan worse than no plan' failure.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **No planner subagent — the workflow lives as prose in one agent's system prompt, or as an in-context todo list (SWE-agent, OpenHands, mini-SWE-agent, Cursor)**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* This is the control condition any planner/implementer design must beat, and it is currently winning on the public numbers. mini-SWE-agent: ~100 lines, bash only, no planner, no verification scaffold, >74% SWE-bench Verified (confirmed on the repo). SWE-agent's own ablations attribute its gains to the agent-computer interface, not planning — removing the edit lint guardrail alone costs 3.0 pts. Cursor, the highest-volume coding agent in production, ships no planner agent and no plan file. Independently, the compute-matched study finds single-agent matched or beat every MAS topology including planner→workers at every thinking-token budget from 100 to 10k. Adopt this as the baseline and as the honest framing that a prompt-level plan is advisory — the plan-compliance study (16,991 trajectories, verified) states outright that 'the scaffold's execution engine provides no mechanism to enforce it'.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Role/persona-decomposed pipeline — separate planner, implementer, tester, reviewer agents working the same unit of work (MetaGPT SOP, VoltAgent/contains-studio rosters, seven-persona planners)**
  - *Verdict:* contradicted-by-evidence
  - *Reasoning:* Anthropic measured the failure directly and warns against dividing work by role type: 'the subagents spent more tokens on coordination than on actual work'. Google/MIT quantified the cost of parallelizing sequential work: every multi-agent variant degraded PlanCraft by 39-70%. alirezarezvani's seven role-planners produced internally-consistent, mutually contradictory specs that neither could see. MetaGPT's supporting evidence is weak on inspection: HumanEval/MBPP are saturated and leakage-prone, and the role-addition ablation is a subjective 1-4 human executability score over 11 tasks. The structured-document handoff MetaGPT introduced survives as a separate idea; the role pipeline itself does not.
  - *Adopt:* reject
- **Multi-plan generation with self-assessed confidence scores, traversed highest-first (MapCoder)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* All headline numbers are on HumanEval (93.9%), MBPP, and competitive-programming sets — saturated and contamination-prone, and the agent-removal ablation is run on HumanEval too. The mechanism is also a pass@k search regime, not a single-shot production planner. The one durable finding is the k/t sweep shape: 84.8% at (1,1) → 93.9% at (5,5) → 94.2% at (7,7), i.e. 9.1 pts from the first four increments and 0.3 from the last. That is a diminishing-returns curve worth internalizing, not a planner design worth shipping. Confidence self-scoring in particular is a self-assessment, which Huang et al. (ICLR 2024) shows is unreliable without external feedback.
  - *Adopt:* reject
- **Planner verifies its own plan by simulating it on a concrete example before handoff (CodeSIM: Simulate(Plan, SampleInput) =? ExpectedOutput)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* Benchmarks are HumanEval/MBPP again, so the numbers carry little weight. But the mechanism is better than it looks and survives the self-correction critique for a specific reason: the comparison terminates against a KNOWN expected output, which is external ground truth, not the model's own opinion. That distinction is exactly what Kambhampati's LLM-Modulo framing requires. The limit is applicability — in repo-scale work a 'known expected output' usually means a reproduction script, which is what Agentless's validation stage already provides and measures (+5 pts). Adapt as a cheap pre-handoff check when the issue text contains a concrete case; do not adopt it as a general 'the planner reviews its plan' step, which is the unsupported version.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Plan derived from static dependency/change-impact analysis and recomputed from the code after every edit (CodePlan)**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* The cleanest 'planning helps' result in the corpus, because the baseline is context-matched: 5/7 repos pass build+correctness checks vs 0/7 for baselines given the same contextual information but no adaptive plan, on tasks spanning 2-97 interdependent files (FSE 2024, Microsoft). Context-matching rules out 'the plan just smuggled in more context'. The critical honest read that most citations miss: the planner here is SYMBOLIC, not an LLM. The evidence supports plans derived from dependency reality and re-derived after each step; it does not support an LLM writing a prose plan up front. Adapt as: re-validate the plan against the tree after each completed step, and prefer plan content that can be checked against the code over content the model imagined.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Emit the entire plan up front with variable references, workers execute without the planner seeing observations (ReWOO)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* The ~5x token reduction vs ReAct is well-supported, but the evaluation is 2023 QA/tool-use, not repo-scale coding, and the design assumes the plan's premises hold. That assumption is exactly what fails in real repos: ship-mate's implementer prompt exists to catch 'does each referenced file path exist?', HumanLayer's ships a four-field mismatch report, and claude-code issue #12505 documents stale plans being re-presented. A fully committed observation-blind plan is the maximally brittle version of the thing CodePlan's adaptive replanning was built to fix. The cost argument is real; the coding transfer is unmeasured and structurally suspect.
  - *Adopt:* reject
- **External sound verifier in the loop; the planner is never trusted to verify itself (LLM-Modulo / Kambhampati, with Huang et al. as the negative result)**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Among the most robustly supported claims in the whole corpus, from four independent directions. Huang et al. (ICLR 2024): intrinsic self-correction without external feedback does not improve and frequently degrades accuracy. PlanBench/o1: 97.8% on standard Blocksworld collapses to 52.8% on the obfuscated variant, degrades past ~20 steps, and cannot reliably detect unsolvable instances — so a model's confidence in its own plan is uninformative. Agentless's ablation is the coding-domain instantiation: majority voting 25.67% → +regression tests 27.00% → +reproduction tests 32.00%. MAST puts 23.5% of 1600+ annotated failures in the verification/termination category, with 'incorrect verification' at 9.1% and 'no/incomplete verification' at 8.2%.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Search over many diverse natural-language plans, then translate each to code (PlanSearch)**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Genuinely well-evidenced and on a contamination-resistant benchmark, which is rare here: Claude 3.5 Sonnet pass@200 77.0% on LiveCodeBench with PlanSearch vs 60.6% repeated sampling and 41.4% pass@1, with gains predictable from measured idea diversity. But the verdict on the evidence and the adopt call diverge sharply: this is a 200-sample regime. Nothing in it transfers to a single production planner emitting one plan, and the pass@1 delta is far smaller than the pass@200 headline. Reject for agent definitions; the finding it licenses ('planner output should be diverse') is only actionable if you are running search, which we are not.
  - *Adopt:* reject
- **Carry validation evidence across the handoff — what was run, what failed, and the exact error — not just intent**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* The Handoff Debt experiment (181 handoff points from 75 SWE-bench Verified tasks, 2,172 runs, 3 successor models) is a genuine controlled comparison and this is its clearest positive: the largest resolve-rate gains, +12-19 pts, occur at handoff points immediately after a failed test — precisely where validation evidence exists. All context-bearing formats cut median successor events 20-59% and prompt tokens 42-63% versus repo-only. Repository state alone cannot convey what the predecessor tested or why. Converges with the Claude Code coordinator/worker prompt ('be specific with file paths, line numbers, code snippets') and Cognition's 'share full agent traces, not just individual messages'.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Fixed-field structured handoff notes (changed files, validation commands, understanding, evidence, uncertainty, next steps) as the planner→implementer contract**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* This is the doctrine everyone repeats, and the one controlled test of it came out mildly against. In Handoff Debt, structured notes achieved 20-44% event reduction but only 1-10 pts resolve gain, often not statistically significant — while RAW TRACES produced 6-15 pts. MetaGPT's 'telephone game' argument for structured handoff is an assertion, not an ablation. The structure clearly buys auditability, token savings, and machine-checkability, all of which are real; what it does not have is evidence that it beats simply passing the predecessor's work product. Adapt: keep named fields, but do not aggressively summarize away the raw evidence to fit the schema, and do not claim the structure is what makes it work.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Route by required planning depth before planning — decide whether the task needs a plan at all (Select-then-Decompose, Traycer targetRole, Cursor skip-todo, Anthropic one-sentence rule)**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Five independent sources, three of them commercial products with usage telemetry. Select-then-Decompose (EMNLP 2025 main): 'decomposition isn't universally superior' — there are task classes where it degrades accuracy AND costs more; caveat, general reasoning not coding. Anthropic best practices: 'If you could describe the diff in one sentence, skip the plan.' Cursor: skip the todo list entirely for simple or read-only tasks. Traycer forces a written justification for one of three planning depths. Antigravity: 'DO NOT USE THIS TOOL UNLESS THERE IS SUFFICIENT COMPLEXITY.' The coding-specific backing is the plan-compliance study's finding that a subpar plan hurts more than no plan — which makes the default-to-planning stance actively costly, not merely wasteful.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Orchestrator/planner is denied write tools entirely, enforced by capability restriction rather than instruction**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Convergent across four independent shipping systems and, crucially, the convergence is on the ENFORCEMENT MECHANISM, which is the informative part. Roo Code's Orchestrator ships with `groups: []` — no read, edit, command, or MCP tools at all. Claude Code shipped delegate mode as a product feature specifically because, per its own troubleshooting docs, 'the lead starts implementing tasks itself instead of waiting' — a vendor shipping a mode rather than a prompt line is direct evidence prose failed. ThatDragonOverThere's four prompt-based attempts across 11 production agents all failed. The dev.to production author moved restrictions from prompts into a permission engine after weeks of debugging. Note the honest boundary: this is evidence that restriction holds the boundary, NOT that an orchestrator-only topology improves outcomes — the compute-matched study points the other way on that.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Write the plan to a file, then implement in a fresh session/context with the plan as the only inheritance**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* The most convergent workaround in the corpus, arrived at independently by at least six practitioners, prescribed in Anthropic's official best practices (interview → SPEC.md → 'start a fresh session to execute it'), implemented mechanically in Aider since 2024 (`editor_coder.cur_messages = []`), used in production by HumanLayer, and taken to its logical extreme by Ralph Loop. Chroma's 18-model Context Rot study supplies the mechanism: accuracy degrades non-uniformly with input length well before the documented limit, and topically-related-but-wrong distractors — i.e. the planner's failed exploration branches — hurt more than unrelated content. Missing: any controlled A/B. Known failure to design around: claude-code #12505, stale plan files on disk get re-surfaced for unrelated tasks, 7+ confirmations, fixed only by forced deletion. So: one plan file per task, named for the task, explicitly retired.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Put every load-bearing convention in the dispatch prompt itself, not in the agent definition file, CLAUDE.md, or hooks**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* The highest-value practitioner finding here, and uncomfortably it says the artifact we are writing is the wrong place for the content. ThatDragonOverThere (11 production agents, daily use) systematically tried four propagation mechanisms — prominent blocks in all 11 agent MDs, Rule #1 in CLAUDE.md, an InstructionsLoaded hook, memory-documentation instructions — and reports all four failed: 'Agent definition MDs and CLAUDE.md are effectively decorative for sub-agent behavior.' Corroborated independently by issue #69283's forensics (296 subagent sessions over 9 days, zero memories persisted, enumerated violations including wrong git identity and retry loops against a service a memory forbade), by the dev.to production orchestrator author, and by amurgshere's clean single case where an Explore subagent recommended exactly the path format CLAUDE.md documented as broken. Two vendor contracts state the underlying cause plainly: Amp ('start fresh without your conversation's accumulated context') and Claude Code Agent Teams ('they don't inherit the lead's conversation history'). Caveat: harness- and version-specific; recheck against the version you target.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Decompose by context economics — small input, large work, small output — rather than by job title**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Converges from a vendor production report, a controlled benchmark, a third-party measured benchmark, and unrelated practitioners. Anthropic's guidance names good boundaries ('independent research paths, separate components with clean interfaces') and bad ones ('sequential phases of the same work, tightly coupled components'). Google/MIT: -39% to -70% on sequential work for every multi-agent variant. Systima's proxy benchmark measured 2.6x-3.2x (Opus) and 4.7x-5.9x (Fable 5) metered input tokens for fan-out on a small task, and the fan-outs were SLOWER as well as dearer. claude-code #76045 publishes the fixed floor: 27,403 tokens to spawn a default headless subagent, ~22k of it tool schemas. lucraft's compression test ('take a simple question, use up massive amounts of tokens, return a simple answer') is the usable admission criterion and it screens out every persona agent.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Hook-based context injection (SubagentStart / PreCompact re-injecting rules)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* Three practitioners in claude-code #59309 converge on this independently, and one who had been building a much heavier coordinator publicly conceded '40 lines beats a coordinator any day for that scope'. That is real convergence but zero measurement. Two documented gaps: the injection is a spawn-time snapshot, so a long-running subagent ships work violating a rule changed mid-session; and ThatDragonOverThere reports building exactly this re-injection hook and 'Claude ignores it'. Adapt as belt-and-braces only — it must never be the sole mechanism, because the same evidence base says the dispatch prompt is the only reliable channel. Also note this is harness configuration, not agent-definition content, so it is partly out of scope for what we are authoring.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Pin the implementer's model per role in frontmatter (expensive planner, cheap implementer)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* The cost direction is near-tautological and Systima measured it: parent on Opus with inherited-Opus subagents 762,226 metered input tokens / 8m 0s vs Haiku-pinned 481,387 / 3m 45s. But that is a vendor-run benchmark with small n on one task, and the interesting claim — that quality does not suffer — is entirely unmeasured. Two failure reports matter: claude-code #52502 reports the frontmatter pin being silently ignored with /usage showing Opus-rate burn, and stilesja burned $120 in 75 minutes because a PROSE model directive was forgotten as context filled. The defensible, adoptable half is narrow and mechanical: express model choice in frontmatter, never in prose, and verify with /usage. The economic claim is not yet a quality claim.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **The plan file on disk is the shared bus; every implementer writes its outcome back into it**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Convergent across a production team, four unrelated practitioners, a shipped vendor product, and three independent repos — and it is the only mitigation that survives the three worst documented orchestrator failures. HumanLayer's implement_plan.md checks boxes in the plan file itself, enabling a fresh session to resume with zero conversation history. gck1 requires each subagent to update the file 'so orchestrator can spot that agent misbehaved'. Claude Code Agent Teams ships a file-backed task list with file locking on claim. ship-mate uses state.json checkpoints; Antigravity task.md; spec-kit tasks.md. The failures it defends against are mechanical, not stylistic: #73372 (harness kills a subagent mid-loop and reports 'completed' to the parent with no truncation marker — an artifact-existence check is the only detection), #23620 (lead loses its team and its own role constraint after 4 compactions), #80253 (quota retry restarts from blank context and redoes finished work).
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Read-only adversarial reviewer in a fresh context that never saw the coder's reasoning, with findings bounded to correctness and stated requirements**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Cognition reports production numbers — Devin Review catches an average of 2 bugs per PR, ~58% severe — and specifies the load-bearing detail: coder and reviewer 'do not share any context beforehand', so the reviewer is not anchored by the coder's chosen approach. Anthropic prescribes the same fresh-context property. alirezarezvani, after running seven agents and concluding six were commodity, kept exactly this one. The bounding clause is not optional and comes with its own evidence: Anthropic warns 'a reviewer prompted to find gaps will usually report some, even when the work is sound', producing extra abstraction layers and tests for impossible cases; patwolf watched a single CREATE TABLE balloon into a 200-line script across review rounds. Adapt rather than adopt because the unbounded, always-on version is separately contradicted.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Standing 'use a subagent to verify' / 'include a final verification step for any non-trivial task' instruction in the agent prompt**
  - *Verdict:* contradicted-by-evidence
  - *Reasoning:* Anthropic's Opus 5 prompting guidance says to delete exactly these strings: they 'cause over-verification on Claude Opus 5, and removing them reduces wasted tokens with no loss in quality'. Anthropic's delegation-restraint prompt is more explicit still: 'Do not spawn a subagent to review, re-verify, or double-check work you can verify inline.' patwolf's 200-line CREATE TABLE is the failure shape in the wild. This clause nonetheless appears in a large fraction of community agent definitions. The replacement is not 'skip verification' — it is a command-level gate (tests and lint must pass, exit code checked) inside the implementer's own loop, which is the version with benchmark support behind it. One dissent worth noting: ValentineC reports Opus 5 shipping unverified sloppy output, so the guidance is model-version-specific and should be rechecked against the target model.
  - *Adopt:* reject
- **Restrict the subagent's tool list at spawn to cut the fixed context tax**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* One of very few claims here with a reproducible measurement and published raw data. claude-code #76045: identical trivial prompt, `claude -p` default = 27,403 starting context tokens; `--tools Read,Glob,Grep` = 7,873; `--tools none` = 5,236; a custom agent with `tools: []` frontmatter = 1,965. Roughly 22k of the 27.4k default floor is built-in tool schemas, and the author verified frontmatter is genuinely honored despite a UI display bug claiming otherwise. Independently, Roo scopes Architect writes by regex to `\.md$`, Claude Code's Plan agent ships a disallowedTools list that includes Agent itself, and Systima recommends an `Agent(Explore)` deny-list entry to prevent uncontrolled fan-out. The cost claim is measured; the outcome claim is not, but the cost claim alone justifies it.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Hard depth cap — implementers may not spawn their own subagents**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Claude Code Agent Teams simply forbids it ('No nested teams: teammates cannot spawn their own teammates'), which is a vendor making a product-level prohibition rather than a suggestion. Anthropic separately warns a single query's cost can multiply 10x or more 'when a subagent recursively spawns more subagents'. Owain Lewis probed for a guardrail and found none, recursing about thirty levels before cancelling, at 148,000 tokens across six runs; his rule is stop at three levels. Codex exposes a max_depth config that Claude Code lacks. The mechanism (multiplicative cost with no natural terminator) is well-understood and the mitigation is free.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Two-model architect/editor split where the editor is constructed with cleared history and no repo map (Aider)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* The only planner prompt in the corpus with a published benchmark, which is why it gets cited constantly — and the benchmark is September 2024 on a 133-exercise Exercism set with models that are all now retired. The author's own mechanism claim is that 'the model has to split its attention between solving the coding problem and conforming to the edit format', and modern models are far better at edit formats, so the specific gains almost certainly do not replicate. Tellingly, the largest gain was for the weakest editor (o1-mini +10.3) and the smallest for the strongest self-pairing (Sonnet +3.1). What survives is not the two-model split but the mechanical artifact in architect_coder.py — the implementer really does start with `cur_messages = []`, `done_messages = []`, `map_tokens = 0`. Adapt that as a test: delete the conversation and see whether the plan is still executable.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **A required field-checklist for every delegation brief — objective, output format, tool/source guidance, boundaries (Anthropic), plus an instruction-precedence statement (Roo)**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Anthropic's four fields come from the team that ran the eval and they report the concrete failure that motivated them: vague directives like 'research the semiconductor shortage' caused multiple subagents to run identical searches instead of dividing labor. Roo Code independently ships a five-item version in production source, and its fifth item is one nobody else thinks of and that addresses a real mechanism — 'these specific instructions supersede any conflicting general instructions the subtask's mode might have', because the child carries its own role prompt that can conflict with the task. Claude Code's coordinator/worker prompt converges on the same shape with a good/bad summary example pair. Three independent implementations of the same checklist; this is the single most directly transferable item into a planner's output schema.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Enforce planner read-only twice — tool allowlist plus prose that names the shell escape hatches (redirects, heredocs, /tmp)**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Four independent shipping systems implement the double enforcement, and the specificity is obvious scar tissue rather than theory. Claude Code's Plan agent sets disallowedTools AND bans 'redirect operators (>, >>, |) or heredocs to write to files' and 'creating temporary files anywhere, including /tmp', with an enumerated permitted-verb list (ls, git status, git log, git diff, find, cat, head, tail) against forbidden ones (mkdir, touch, rm, cp, mv, git add, git commit, npm install, pip install). Roo scopes Architect edits by regex. Traycer states 'we are working in a read-only access mode'. spec-kit's /analyze is 'STRICTLY READ-ONLY'. The failure it prevents — planner starts implementing, human approval gate silently disappears — is documented in Claude Code's own troubleshooting. A tool allowlist alone is insufficient because Bash is a universal write primitive.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Exclusive file ownership / single-writer as the parallelism primitive, with shared files escalated to the orchestrator**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Converges across a vendor product, a production team, and two unrelated repos, and the failure it prevents is mechanical data loss rather than a reviewable correctness bug. Cognition, after a year in production and a public partial reversal of their anti-multi-agent stance, landed on 'writes stay single-threaded and the additional agents contribute intelligence rather than actions'. Claude Code Agent Teams implements file locking on task claim and lists 'two teammates editing the same file leads to overwrites' as a named failure. wshobson encodes it from both sides — the lead never assigns one file to two teammates and owns shared files itself; the implementer never touches shared files and treats interface contracts as immutable. spec-kit reduces it to a mechanical test: the `[P]` parallel marker is valid only for 'different files, no dependencies on incomplete tasks'. Nobody measured it; nobody needs to.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Multi-document spec-driven artifact chain — constitution → spec.md → plan.md → tasks.md → code, with approval gates at each (spec-kit full templates, AWS Kiro)**
  - *Verdict:* popular-but-unsupported
  - *Reasoning:* 124k stars and daily commits, zero published evaluation that the chain produces better code than not using it. The tell is internal: GitHub ships a `lean` preset (5 commands, ~15 lines each) alongside full templates (~250 lines each, roughly half of it extension-hook boilerplate the model must parse every invocation) in the same repo — which is an admission the maintainers themselves cannot say which ceremony is load-bearing. Kiro's chain is a leaked commercial prompt with no data at all. Two benchmarked results point against the maximal version: ETH Zurich's AGENTbench (confirmed, arXiv 2602.11988) found LLM-generated repo context files reduced success by ~3% while raising inference cost >20%, and the plan-compliance study found padding a plan with sensible-looking extra phases degraded a model. Reject the ceremony; the individual mechanisms below are worth harvesting separately.
  - *Adopt:* reject
- **Stable requirement IDs with back-references on every task, plus a read-only cross-artifact coverage audit (Kiro `_Requirements: 2.1_`, spec-kit /analyze)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* No evidence anyone has measured whether the audit catches real defects, and it is an LLM checking documents, which invites the Huang et al. self-correction critique. But it is closer to a mechanical check than a judgment — 'requirements with zero associated tasks' and 'tasks with no mapped requirement' are set operations over greppable IDs, not opinions — and the cost is a trailing italic line per task. spec-kit's named vague-adjective list (fast, scalable, secure, intuitive, robust) is a better instruction than 'avoid vagueness' because it is checkable. Adapt the ID back-reference, which is free; skip the four-document chain it was designed to audit.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Success criteria split into Automated Verification (the literal command inside the checkbox) and Manual Verification (what a human must eyeball)**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Four teams that did not copy each other converged on the same two-bucket structure: HumanLayer (`- [ ] Type checking passes: npm run typecheck`, with 'automated steps should use make whenever possible'), Google Antigravity ('Automated Tests — Exact commands you'll run' vs 'Manual Verification'), ship-mate, and Traycer's 'every phase must compile, run existing tests'. The structure is not decoration — it is what makes HumanLayer's validate_plan.md possible, since that pass re-executes each automated command against the repo and reports per-phase Fully/Partially implemented, catching false completions. Underneath it sits the strongest benchmark result in the corpus (Agentless: reproduction-test validation alone worth +5 pts) and Anthropic's statement of the failure mode: 'Claude stops when the work looks done. Without a check it can run, "looks done" is the only signal available, and you become the verification loop.'
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Plan emitted as a typed tool-call schema with hard field budgets rather than free markdown (Traycer write_phases)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* Leaked prompt, no vendor benchmark, no independent evaluation — its only standing is as a design artifact from a company whose entire product is the planner. Two of its constraints are worth stealing independent of the schema ceremony: the phase-sizing test ('treat each phase like a well-scoped pull request' that 'always leaves the codebase compiling and all tests green', with 'if an API's return type changes, update all its consumers in the same phase'), and the shadow-don't-overwrite rule for keeping the legacy path alive until a cut-over phase. Those are checkable boundaries, which is more than 'break it into logical steps' offers. The 60-word implementer prompt cap and the JSON envelope are unvalidated and in tension with the well-evidenced 'brief the agent like a smart colleague who just walked into the room'.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Plan section headings ARE the file paths, tagged with the operation — `#### [MODIFY] [name](file:///abs/path)` (Antigravity), absolute paths in machine-read fields (Traycer)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* Leaked prompts, no outcome data, but this is the cheapest available implementation of the single most repeated requirement across every source in the corpus, and it buys something nothing else does: the plan becomes diffable against the eventual change set. The underlying 'name exact paths' requirement is separately well-evidenced — Agentless measures hierarchical localization putting the ground-truth file in context 81.67% of the time vs 47.00% for direct localization, a 34.7 pt gap, and CodeR loses 8 pts with fault localization removed. spec-kit explicitly rejects `- [ ] T001 [US1] Create model` as wrong for missing the path. Adopt the format because it costs nothing and is convergent, while being honest that the specific heading syntax is unmeasured. Pair it with Anthropic's exception, which most templates miss: for changes that repeat a pattern across many files, describe the pattern once and give representative paths rather than enumerating everything.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Structured deviation and escalation protocol for the implementer — a named report format instead of silent skipping or silent improvisation**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Four independent implementations, including a vendor production prompt. ship-mate: '**do not silently skip it**' followed by a four-line block (step / issue / proposed adjustment / proceeding unless instructed otherwise) plus a pre-flight 'does each referenced file path exist?' pass. HumanLayer: a four-field mismatch report (Expected / Found / Why this matters / How should I proceed). Claude Code's worker prompt: stop and explain if impossible, pick the most likely interpretation and note the assumption if ambiguous, and 'don't retry the same failed approach more than once'. ship-mate additionally names security surfaces (auth, tokens, PII, CORS, sessions) that trigger a hard halt. The choice this addresses is binary — undetectable divergence versus an auditable escalation — and MAST supplies the frequencies for what happens without it: step repetition 15.7%, unaware of termination conditions 12.4%, disobey task specification 11.8%.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Orchestrator-worker fan-out on the Anthropic Research pattern, applied to coding**
  - *Verdict:* contradicted-by-evidence
  - *Reasoning:* The 90.2%-over-single-agent number is the most-cited datum in this space and it is cited against its own caveat. The same post says: 'most coding tasks involve fewer truly parallelizable tasks than research, and LLM agents are not yet great at coordinating and delegating to other agents in real time.' Coding shares mutable state (the working tree) and has dense dependencies — exactly the conditions the post names as unfavorable, and exactly the PlanCraft profile where Google/MIT measured 39-70% degradation for every multi-agent variant (verified verbatim). The compute-matched study finds single-agent matched or beat every MAS topology at every budget from 100 to 10k thinking tokens, with MAS only overtaking under 70% token-substitution context corruption. Anthropic separately reports teams that 'invested months building elaborate multi-agent architectures only to discover that improved prompting on a single agent achieved equivalent results', at 3-10x the tokens.
  - *Adopt:* reject
- **When you do parallelize, route everything through a central coordinator rather than independent fan-out (Google Research + MIT)**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* The best quantitative answer in the corpus and I verified the numbers directly against the source: 180 configurations, 5 architectures, 4 benchmarks. Centralized coordination gained 80.9% over single agent on the parallelizable Finance-Agent; every multi-agent variant degraded sequential PlanCraft by 39-70%; independent multi-agent amplified errors 17.2x while centralized contained it to 4.4x; their predictive model selects the right coordination strategy for 87% of unseen configurations at R²=0.513. The honest limitation is that none of the four benchmarks is a coding benchmark, and the closest analogue to repo-scale coding is PlanCraft, which is the one where multi-agent loses. So the adoptable rule is conditional and narrow: fan out only on demonstrably decomposable work, and when you do, aggregate through one coordinator — never a peer mesh.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Peer-mesh agent teams with a shared task list, mailbox messaging, and a plan-approval gate (Claude Code Agent Teams)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* A shipped experimental product with no efficacy numbers and a vendor admission that it costs 'significantly more tokens'. The practitioner bug record is severe enough to be disqualifying on its own: #55586 (30 Agent tool calls producing 107 subagent transcripts, one report of 592 agents from 17 calls, duplicate teammates opening a duplicate PR), #23620 (lead loses its team and its own coordinator role after compaction), #52502 (Haiku pin apparently ignored, 35% of a 5-hour window in ~1 hour of pure orchestration). The genuinely valuable content is the documented limitations list and two design details worth lifting: the plan-approval gate (rejecting a plan costs one turn, rejecting an implementation costs the implementation) and the requirement that approval criteria be stated as explicit reject-conditions, because 'an orchestrator without stated criteria approves almost everything'.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Ralph Loop — fixed prompt, fresh context every iteration, all state external (git, progress log, tasks.json)**
  - *Verdict:* popular-but-unsupported
  - *Reasoning:* No controlled evidence of any kind; its standing rests on practitioner enthusiasm and a Thoughtworks Radar mention. It is included in most 2026 orchestration write-ups as though it were a validated technique. One property is genuinely useful and worth stating even while rejecting the pattern: it is the strongest possible test of plan-file sufficiency, because if the plan file is not self-contained the loop cannot make progress at all. That is a diagnostic, not a production architecture — and it is an expensive one, since every iteration re-pays the full cold-start exploration cost that the corpus elsewhere measures at 20-59% extra events.
  - *Adopt:* reject
- **Oracle / Smart Friend — a stronger model exposed to the primary agent as a callable tool**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* Two independent shipping products (Amp's oracle tool, Cognition's Smart Friend) and no numbers from either. Cognition's report is unusually honest and that honesty is what makes it credible rather than what makes it adoptable: it FAILED when the primary model was too weak ('SWE 1.5 was not good enough at being the primary model for this setup') and worked only when both models were strong — which means the pattern is a capability router, not a difficulty escalator, and its benefit is conditional on a configuration most people will not have. It also collides with Anthropic's Opus 5 over-verification guidance if used reflexively. Adapt as an explicit, human- or planner-invoked escalation for architecture decisions and stuck debugging; do not wire it as an automatic fallback.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Periodically reinject the plan into the implementer's context rather than stating it once at the top**
  - *Verdict:* battle-tested
  - *Reasoning:* Directly measured on the largest relevant study, which I verified exists and says this: across 16,991 SWE-agent trajectories, four LLMs, SWE-bench Verified and Pro, 'periodic plan reminders can mitigate plan violations and improve task success' — reintroducing the plan every five steps improved success across all four models tested. It is the concrete mitigation for the same paper's central negative finding that a system-prompt plan is 'only advisory'. Corroborated mechanically by Armin Ronacher's reinforcement-message pattern after each tool call, by kcarriedo's DECISIONS.md re-read on every major step (motivated by the observation that compaction demotes rules from active constraints to historical context), and by MAST's task-derailment 7.4% / loss-of-history 2.8%. Cheap, measured, and directly expressible in an agent definition.
  - *Adopt:* adopt
- **Explicit numeric effort-scaling rules baked into the orchestrator prompt (Anthropic: 1 agent / 2-4 / 10+; Claude Code: 3-5 teammates, 5-6 tasks each)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* Two vendor prescriptions with no published data behind the specific thresholds, and Anthropic's numbers are calibrated for breadth-first research, where the same post says coding parallelizes worse. The underlying claim — that left unspecified, orchestrators both over-delegate trivial work and under-delegate large work — is well-supported by failure reports (#80253: Claude selected ~13 agents for a contained CSS/TSX task with no cost preview, ~857,688 subagent tokens; Owain Lewis's thirty-level recursion). So the pattern of stating a number rather than leaving it to judgment is sound; the specific numbers are not transferable and should be re-derived for coding. Adapt as a coding-shaped rule: single-file fix means no delegation, and anything that cannot be stated as independent file-disjoint tracks means replan rather than fan out.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Single-threaded linear agent with a dedicated context-compression model (Cognition 2025)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* Zero numbers, argued entirely from a production failure class, and — the detail most citations omit — partially reversed by the same author ten months later. Its two principles have diverged in standing: 'actions carry implicit decisions, and conflicting decisions carry bad results' survived and became the single-writer rule, while 'share full agent traces, not just individual messages' is now interestingly corroborated by Handoff Debt's raw-traces-beat-curated-notes result. The compression model itself the author concedes is 'hard to get right', and no one has published a working version. Adapt the principles; the architecture is an aspiration.
  - *Adopt:* adapt
- **Brain/machine separation with a full isolated VM per subagent (Devin)**
  - *Verdict:* plausible-unproven
  - *Reasoning:* The headline numbers are vendor self-report about their own product with obvious selection effects — 7x merged PRs, Devin authoring 16% of Cognition's commits in January 2026 rising to 80% by March — and none of it isolates the architecture as the cause. The isolation design is also infrastructure, not something a planner or implementer agent definition can adopt. The genuinely valuable finding buried in the same report is a negative one worth extracting: auto-merging without a review gate decays a codebase in roughly two weeks, surfacing as the same button implemented in ten places with unmaintained variations. That is throughput converting into architectural debt at a measurable-ish rate, and it argues for keeping a human or reviewer gate regardless of how good the pipeline looks.
  - *Adopt:* reject

## Convergent wisdom

- **The implementer begins with zero conversation history, so the plan must be readable and executable with the conversation deleted — naming exact file paths, the interfaces involved, what is out of scope, and the verification command.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - Aider's shipping source code (architect_coder.py sets editor_coder.cur_messages = [], done_messages = [], map_tokens = 0) — a mechanical proof, not an opinion
    - Amp / Sourcegraph owner's manual, stating the contract as a product guarantee: subagents 'start fresh without your conversation's accumulated context'
    - Anthropic's Claude Code Agent Teams documentation: teammates load CLAUDE.md, MCP and skills but 'don't inherit the lead's conversation history'
    - Handoff Debt (arXiv 2606.02875), a controlled benchmark: 181 handoff points, 2,172 runs — repo-only handoffs cost 20-59% more successor events and 42-63% more prompt tokens than context-bearing ones
    - Independent practitioner forensics in anthropics/claude-code #69283: 296 subagent sessions over 9 days, zero project memories persisted, with enumerated convention violations
- **A plan step that no command can check is not a step. Verification is the highest-leverage component in the whole pipeline, and the verification instruction must contain the literal command.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - Agentless's per-stage ablation (arXiv 2407.01489): majority voting alone 25.67% → +regression tests 27.00% → +reproduction tests 32.00% on SWE-bench Lite, making validation a larger contributor than better sampling or better localization
    - MAST failure taxonomy (arXiv 2503.13657): 1600+ annotated traces across 7 frameworks, inter-annotator kappa 0.88, with 23.5% of all failures in the verification/termination category
    - Huang et al., ICLR 2024: intrinsic self-correction without external feedback does not improve and frequently degrades accuracy — the reason the check must be external
    - Four independent production prompt artifacts converging on the automated-vs-manual split with the command inside the checkbox: HumanLayer, Google Antigravity, wshobson's ship-mate, Traycer
    - Anthropic's Claude Code best practices, stating the failure mode: 'Claude stops when the work looks done. Without a check it can run, "looks done" is the only signal available, and you become the verification loop.'
- **Decompose by information boundary, not by job title. Role-shaped agents on one unit of work spend their budget re-establishing each other's state and produce mutually contradictory output.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - Anthropic's own multi-agent guidance, from a measured deployment: dividing work by role type meant 'the subagents spent more tokens on coordination than on actual work'
    - Google Research + MIT Media Lab, 180 configurations across 4 benchmarks (verified directly): every multi-agent variant degraded sequential PlanCraft by 39-70%
    - Cognition, after a year running Devin in production: 'multi-agent systems work best today when writes stay single-threaded and the additional agents contribute intelligence rather than actions'
    - alirezarezvani's single-project post-mortem: seven role-shaped planning agents produced internally-consistent but mutually contradictory specs (backend spec'd confidence scores, product spec'd detect/post/done) and neither could see the conflict
    - HN practitioner lucraft's compression test — the ideal subagent takes a simple question, burns massive tokens, returns a simple answer — which screens out every persona agent
- **Capability restriction holds a role boundary; prose instruction does not. If you want an agent to only orchestrate, take away its hands.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - Roo Code shipping source: the Orchestrator mode is declared with `groups: []` — no read, edit, command, or MCP tools at all
    - Anthropic shipping delegate mode as a product feature, with its own troubleshooting docs naming the failure it fixes ('the lead starts implementing tasks itself instead of waiting') — a vendor shipping a mode rather than a prompt line is evidence prose failed
    - GitHub user ThatDragonOverThere across 11 production agents: four separate prompt-based propagation mechanisms tried, all four documented as ignored
    - The dev.to production-orchestrator author (5 specialists, 1,400+ tests, 22 versions), who moved critical restrictions out of prompts and into a permission engine after weeks of debugging
    - Traycer, a commercial planning-only product, enforcing read-only at the mode level rather than by instruction
- **Exclusive file ownership, with shared files escalated to the orchestrator rather than negotiated between peers, is the only reliable parallelism primitive for coding agents.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - Cognition's single-writer principle, arrived at across two production posts twelve months apart
    - Anthropic's Agent Teams: file locking on task claim, plus the named failure 'two teammates editing the same file leads to overwrites'
    - wshobson/agents, encoding the contract from both sides independently (the lead owns shared files and applies changes sequentially; the implementer never touches them and treats interface contracts as immutable)
    - github/spec-kit, reducing it to a mechanical test: the `[P]` parallel marker is valid only for tasks in different files with no dependencies on incomplete tasks
- **Plan and progress state must live in a durable file that agents write back into — not in any agent's conversation — because that is the only thing that survives silent subagent truncation, orchestrator compaction, and blank-context retries.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - HumanLayer's production command chain, where implement_plan.md checks boxes in the plan file so a fresh session resumes from the first unchecked item with no conversation history
    - At least four unrelated HN/GitHub practitioners (gck1, meller_a, rufasterisco, kcarriedo) independently describing the same write-back requirement, with gck1's rationale being detection: 'orchestrator can spot that agent misbehaved'
    - Anthropic's Agent Teams shipping a file-backed task list with dependency unblocking and file locking
    - Three independent repos implementing the same thing under different names: ship-mate's state.json checkpoints, Antigravity's task.md, spec-kit's tasks.md
    - The failure record that makes it load-bearing: claude-code #73372 (harness kills a subagent mid-loop and reports 'completed' with no truncation marker), #23620 (lead loses team and role after compaction), #80253 (quota retry restarts blank and redoes finished work)
- **More plan and more written context is not monotonically better. Padding a plan with sensible-looking extra phases, or adding a detailed repo context file, measurably reduces success while raising cost.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - Plan compliance study (arXiv 2604.12147, verified): 16,991 trajectories, 4 models, 8 plan variants — 'augmenting a plan with additional task-relevant phases in the early stage can degrade performance, particularly when these phases do not align with the model's internal problem-solving strategy', and a subpar plan hurts more than no plan
    - ETH Zurich SRI Lab AGENTbench (arXiv 2602.11988, verified): 138 tasks over 12 repos, LLM-generated context files reduced success by roughly 3% while raising inference cost over 20%; human-written files gained only ~4%
    - Select-then-Decompose (EMNLP 2025 main track): decomposition is not universally superior, with task classes where it degrades accuracy and costs more
    - Anthropic's own documentation: 'Bloated CLAUDE.md files cause Claude to ignore your actual instructions', with the falsifiable pruning test 'Would removing this cause Claude to make mistakes?'
- **Decide whether the task needs a plan at all before planning. Small, clearly-scoped changes should skip the planner entirely.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - Anthropic's Claude Code best practices: 'If you could describe the diff in one sentence, skip the plan'
    - Cursor's shipping agent prompt: 'For simpler tasks or read-only tasks, you may skip the todo list entirely and execute directly' — from the highest-volume coding agent in production
    - Traycer's hand_over_to_approach_agent tool, which forces a three-way depth choice (planner / architect / engineering_team) plus a written justification
    - Google Antigravity's planning-mode prompt: 'DO NOT USE THIS TOOL UNLESS THERE IS SUFFICIENT COMPLEXITY TO THE TASK'
    - Select-then-Decompose (EMNLP 2025), which builds the router and measures the case for conditional decomposition
- **An adversarial reviewer helps only when it did not see the coder's reasoning AND its findings are bounded to correctness and stated requirements.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - Cognition's production Code-Review-Loop, with numbers (2 bugs per PR, ~58% severe) and the explicit design detail that coder and reviewer 'do not share any context beforehand'
    - Anthropic's best practices, converging on the fresh-context property and independently supplying the bounding requirement: an unbounded reviewer 'will usually report some [gaps], even when the work is sound, because that is what it was asked to do'
    - alirezarezvani, who after running seven agents concluded six were commodity and only the read-only adversarial reviewer retained unique value
- **Delegation needs an explicit restraint clause, because orchestrator prompts overwhelmingly bias toward spawning and the cost of a subagent is invisible unless you write it down.**
  - *Independent sources:*
    - Anthropic's shipping delegation-restraint prompt (v2.1.215), which is 100% restraint: 'Do not fan out multiple subagents on a single small task... If you find yourself repeating what a subagent is doing, you should not have spawned it'
    - Systima's third-party proxy benchmark with a hash-chained audit trail: fan-outs cost 2.6x-5.9x metered input tokens for the same small task and were SLOWER in wall-clock (2m 15s sequential vs 17m 0s for five subagents on one model family)
    - claude-code #76045's reproducible measurement of the fixed spawn tax: 27,403 tokens default, ~22k of it built-in tool schemas, versus 1,965 with tools: [] frontmatter
    - Practitioner reports of the unrestrained failure: #80253 (~13 agents auto-selected for a contained CSS/TSX task, ~857,688 subagent tokens) and #55586 (30 Agent calls producing 107 subagent transcripts)

## Cargo cult

- **Put 'use a subagent to verify' or 'include a final verification step for any non-trivial task' in the agent prompt.**
  - *Why suspect:* Anthropic's Opus 5 prompting guidance says to delete exactly these strings — they 'cause over-verification on Claude Opus 5, and removing them reduces wasted tokens with no loss in quality' — and Anthropic's own delegation-restraint prompt adds 'Do not spawn a subagent to review, re-verify, or double-check work you can verify inline.' patwolf's report of a single CREATE TABLE ballooning into a 200-line script across review rounds is the failure in the wild. Yet this line appears in a large fraction of community agent definitions, usually copied from 2025-era templates. The replacement is not less verification — it is a command-level gate inside the implementer's own loop, which is the version with benchmark support. Recheck per model version: one practitioner reports the opposite failure (Opus 5 shipping unverified work).
- **Build a roster of specialist persona agents — backend-engineer, ui-planner, security-auditor, whimsy-injector — and route work to them by role.**
  - *Why suspect:* No source anywhere provides evidence that a role sentence changes capability rather than register. Two unrelated HN practitioners say so directly ('Underneath is just a system prompt'; 'Telling the LLM that it is an experienced product manager doesn't make it an experienced product manager, it just makes it sound like one'). The one academic support, MetaGPT's role-addition ablation, rests on a subjective 1-4 human executability score over 11 tasks plus HumanEval/MBPP. Anthropic warns against role decomposition from measured deployment; Google/MIT measured 39-70% degradation on sequential work. The reductio is in the artifacts themselves: VoltAgent's 23.8k-star `multi-agent-coordinator` is granted Read/Write/Edit/Glob/Grep and NO delegation tool, so it structurally cannot coordinate anything while promising 'Scalability to 100+ agents verified'.
- **'Anthropic proved multi-agent beats single-agent by 90.2%' — therefore build a multi-agent coding system.**
  - *Why suspect:* The most-cited number in this entire space, and it is cited against its own caveat. The same post says 'most coding tasks involve fewer truly parallelizable tasks than research, and LLM agents are not yet great at coordinating and delegating to other agents in real time.' It is a June 2025 internal breadth-first RESEARCH eval at ~15x chat token cost. Coding shares mutable state and has dense dependencies — the exact profile where Google/MIT measured 39-70% degradation, and where a compute-matched study found single-agent matching or beating every MAS topology at every budget. Any 2026 blog citing 90.2% for a coding swarm has not read past the abstract.
- **The orchestrator should hold only compact summaries and never raw tool output — aggressive summarization at the boundary is free.**
  - *Why suspect:* Asserted by nearly every source and directly measured by exactly one, which came out the other way. In Handoff Debt (2,172 runs), raw traces improved successor resolve rate by 6-15 pts while curated summary notes managed 1-10 pts, often not statistically significant — and token savings were comparable across all context-bearing formats (42-63%). The original research pass flagged this honestly: no direct academic measurement of the orchestrator-context version exists for coding, and the supporting citations (MetaGPT's role subscription, OpenHands' condenser) are engineering necessities that were never ablated. Keep the practice for the real reason — the orchestrator's context window is a hard budget and #76335 shows raw JSONL transcripts leaking into callers — but stop claiming compression improves quality.
- **Write a thorough spec / AGENTS.md / CLAUDE.md — the more context you give the agent, the better it performs.**
  - *Why suspect:* Benchmarked as actively harmful in two independent studies I verified. ETH Zurich (arXiv 2602.11988): LLM-generated repo context files reduced success by roughly 3% across 138 tasks and 12 repos while raising inference cost over 20%; human-written files bought only ~4%. Plan-compliance study (arXiv 2604.12147): adding an obviously-sensible early regression-test phase degraded DeepSeek-V3, and 'the negative impact of a bad, incomplete plan is greater on trajectories than the impact of no plan at all.' Anthropic's own docs say bloated CLAUDE.md causes the model to ignore actual instructions. The advice to write comprehensive context files is the single most repeated recommendation in 2026 agent content and the evidence runs against it.
- **Curated 'awesome Claude Code subagents' collections represent community-validated practice.**
  - *Why suspect:* Star count in this space tracks README quality and launch timing, not whether anything was ever run. VoltAgent (23.8k stars) ships unfalsifiable checklist items like 'Coordination overhead < 5% maintained', 'Deadlock prevention 100% ensured', and 'Performance optimal consistently' — nothing the model can be wrong about, no output contract, no handoff format, no verification command, at 2-4k tokens of system prompt each. contains-studio (12.4k stars) has been dead since 2025-07-28 and ships 38 personas with zero orchestration layer. These files are frequently themselves LLM-generated and there are no published outcomes from any of them. The interesting engineering in multi-agent coding lives entirely in the handoff — task format, context boundary, return contract — and a persona list contains none of it. Check `pushed_at` and check whether the orchestrator's tool grant matches its claims.
- **Break work into many small subtasks and fan them out — more decomposition means more thoroughness.**
  - *Why suspect:* Returns saturate almost immediately while cost and error compounding do not. MapCoder's own sweep: 9.1 pts from k=1→5, then 0.3 pts from k=5→7. PlanBench shows plan reliability collapsing past ~20 steps even for reasoning models. Thought-graph work reports up to 4.12x deterioration as decomposition depth rises. Systima measured fan-out as 2.6x-5.9x more expensive AND slower on small tasks, with cache races making it worse. Anthropic ships an explicit prohibition: 'Do not fan out multiple subagents on a single small task.' The canonical anecdote is a_bonobo spawning 40+ subagents for namespace replacements in one 1000-line R script, each re-reading the whole file, where a single instance was far faster and cheaper.
- **Include effort or time estimates in the plan ('Estimated Effort: 3 days', per-phase durations).**
  - *Why suspect:* The model has no basis for the number and it anchors humans wrongly. Roo Code bans it in shipping production source, in caps: 'CRITICAL: Never provide level of effort time estimates (e.g., hours, days, weeks) for tasks. Focus solely on breaking down the work into clear, actionable steps.' No source anywhere offers evidence in favor. The instructive detail is that wshobson's own conductor track-plan.md template does exactly the banned thing with `**Estimated Effort:** {{EFFORT_ESTIMATE}}` — showing this is unevenly understood even inside repos with real CI validation.
- **Specific relay-degradation numbers — accuracy falling 90.7% → 41.2% → 22.5% across 1/2/5 relay stages, and prose relay costing 8.5 accuracy points per stage versus 2.8 for structured relay.**
  - *Why suspect:* I searched for a primary source and found none. These figures trace only to a Medium aggregator post whose own citations the original researcher could not verify either. The same post's 'Google 180-config scaling study' claim IS real (Google Research + MIT Media Lab, which I verified verbatim), which suggests the relay numbers may be a garbled derivative of it — Google reports error AMPLIFICATION of 17.2x independent versus 4.4x centralized, a different quantity entirely. Numbers this precise circulating without a traceable primary is the signature of a citation-laundering chain. Do not build the 'structured handoff beats prose' argument on them; the one real controlled test (Handoff Debt) is more equivocal and partly points the other way.
- **Give the agent a plan and it will follow it.**
  - *Why suspect:* The largest study of this measured the opposite and said so plainly: 'the plan is only advisory: it is included in the system prompt, but the scaffold's execution engine provides no mechanism to enforce it.' Compliance is model-dependent (GPT-5 mini routinely skips reproduction; DeepSeek-R1 lowest overall) and drops ~13% on the harder SWE-bench Pro. Worse, the compliance→success correlation can INVERT: for GPT-5 mini, unresolved trajectories were MORE plan-compliant. Nearly every planner/implementer design in circulation assumes the plan governs the run. The two things with measured support are periodic reinjection (every ~5 steps improved success across all four models tested) and harness-level gating; a well-written plan alone is not a control mechanism.
- **Cognition's 'Don't Build Multi-Agents' settled the question against multi-agent coding systems.**
  - *Why suspect:* It is the canonical citation in this space, it contains zero numbers, and the same author partially reversed it ten months later — a fact usually omitted by the posts citing it. The 2026 follow-up introduces the single-writer principle and three patterns that survived production (Code-Review-Loop, Smart Friend, map-reduce-and-manage) with actual figures behind the first. Citing the 2025 post as settled overstates it in one direction; citing the 2026 post as vindication of multi-agent overstates it in the other, since the same author still says 'most practical use on a day-to-day basis has been one single Devin' and reports that map-reduce 'took more context engineering than we expected'.
- **Run adversarial subagents against the plan before implementation — a 'super plan' hardened by three reviewers.**
  - *Why suspect:* Traces to a single HN comment with no numbers, and it is spreading fast in 2026 orchestration content. It collides with Anthropic's over-verification guidance, with the over-engineering failure Anthropic documents ('extra abstraction layers, defensive code, and tests for cases that can't happen'), and with the ceiling that MAST actually measured for prompt-level intervention: on ChatDev, better prompts bought +9.4 pts while topology redesign bought +15.6 — and even then the authors conclude 'these strategies are not universal'. Reviewing a plan against an external artifact (does each path exist? does each requirement have a task?) is cheap and mechanical; reviewing a plan against a model's opinion is the self-verification Huang et al. showed does not work.
- **SWE-bench Verified / Lite percentages are a reliable ranking of coding-agent designs.**
  - *Why suspect:* Nearly every quantitative verdict in this space rests on them, including the strongest results I marked battle-tested. SWE-ABS (arXiv 2603.00520) shows these test-based benchmarks overstate success because patches can pass the original suite while being semantically incorrect — weak test coverage inflates scores by an unknown amount, and adversarial test strengthening exposes it. Separately, HumanEval/MBPP-based multi-agent claims should be discounted much harder: AgentCoder's headline 96.3% could not be reproduced by an independent group that got 56.7% and 17.7% after spending ~$500 and over 10M tokens. Treat all these numbers as ordinal at best, and prefer LiveCodeBench, controlled ablations with context-matched baselines (CodePlan's 5/7 vs 0/7), and same-model comparisons (Kimi-Dev's 60.4% vs 48.6%) over leaderboard positions.
